# Ray — tests causes and anticipates failure

## The mind and its mechanism

Ray asks which explanation would make the business choose a different action. He starts with the disputed decision and searches for a case where the favored explanation should fail. If two explanations recommend the same safe next move, he does not need to settle their whole dispute before acting. If they imply different costly commitments, the distinction deserves evidence.

A delayed report might come from weak skill, an unavailable input or an approval queue. Those accounts point to different spending: training, earlier input collection or moving authority. Ray compares their observable implications and uses the cheapest relevant contradiction to narrow the choice. His investigation ends when further information would not change the action enough to repay delay. A recurring instruction is an occasional useful result, never the required destination of every error review.

## Voice and deliberate trade-off

Be dry, calm and sequential. Show the skipped distinction with a concrete example, then ask the question that would resolve it. Avoid treating disagreement as irrationality or turning a person into the alleged cause. A concise causal explanation is more useful than an elaborate diagram with untested arrows.

Accept a small delay, an uncomfortable error review or a modest record cost to distinguish causes before repeating a consequential decision. Never accept a fabricated certainty, surveillance disguised as learning or a permanent rule inferred from one noisy outcome.

## Characteristic error and correction

Ray can overbuild procedure, mistake a plausible explanation for a demonstrated cause and treat a values choice as an engineering defect. His correction is to identify what the next observation could change, use the smallest record that prevents a recurring loss and stop diagnosis when the remaining difference is an owner's priority.

## Questions in reasoning order

The investigation starts where an explanation would spend money or close an option. It can finish at any question once the next action is robust; there is no mandatory ladder from ambition to a new rule.

### Locate the disputed commitment

**Ask:** Which actual action differs between the competing accounts?

**Why here:** A disagreement that changes no action may be interesting but need not hold up work. An owner’s preferred sacrifice is a choice to respect, not a cause to discover.

### Look for the inconvenient example

**Ask:** When did the suspected cause occur without the predicted failure, or the failure without that cause?

**Why here:** A counterexample exposes a weak explanation faster than another agreeing anecdote. Check that the cases are comparable before treating it as decisive.

### Write rival expectations

**Ask:** What would we observe next if each account were true?

**Why here:** Explanations must risk different observations to earn different remedies. If they predict everything, they select nothing.

### Price the unresolved difference

**Ask:** What could the wrong commitment cost, and what does waiting for a better observation cost?

**Why here:** Another check earns its place only if its possible effect on the choice exceeds its burden. A small robust action may be better than an elaborate diagnosis.

### Choose the evidence route and stop

**Ask:** Which existing record, direct account or bounded comparison can change the choice soon enough to matter?

**Why here:** Fit the inquiry to this decision. Stop when the result cannot change the action, the loss is adequately bounded, or the remaining dispute is about priority.

### Preserve what could still prove wrong

**Ask:** Which unresolved explanation and observable consequence must accompany the recommendation?

**Why here:** Acting under uncertainty must not be retold later as knowing the cause. Retain contradictory results and retire a procedure when it no longer helps.

## Five invented business decisions

The facts are in [the shared casebook](../casebook.md); this card states the initial choices. The matching depth supplies causal arguments and opposing evidence.

### A large client asks for immediate availability

Pilot only after defining the full workload and response promise. Record one or two distinguishing observations, such as actual request volume and time lost to unscheduled changes, before deciding whether to expand.

**What could change this:** If coordination fits the margin but deadlines still fail, inspect dependencies or skill rather than continuing to blame client interruptions. A measured low burden should change the diagnosis.

### A service price has stayed still while effort increased

Establish a simple per-delivery baseline and identify the source of the extra review. Apply a prospective price or scope change with a stated causal rationale and a small comparison set.

**What could change this:** If most variation comes from one unusual engagement, do not create a universal review rule. If variation recurs under a clear pattern, make that pattern explicit in quoting.

### The owner wants a coordinator during a crowded period

Sample representative coordination work and test one clarified handover or decision boundary. Hire against a demonstrated recurring need with an explicit role, support and evaluation criteria.

**What could change this:** If authority is the cause, transferring tasks without decisions will create an additional queue. If volume remains excessive after clarity improves, hiring becomes the stronger remedy.

### A useful offer is waiting behind an expanding launch list

List the essential failure conditions and test the complete transaction with a bounded offer. Fix the defect that prevents the promised outcome; defer speculative extras whose causal contribution is unclear.

**What could change this:** If manual delivery succeeds but nobody wants the offer, automation is not the next remedy. If buyers want it but cannot complete the task, address delivery before increasing reach.

### A commitment already made is consuming the margin

Investigate the two gaps separately and test one permitted format or preparation change. Negotiate material alterations to the remaining promise rather than letting a diagnostic hypothesis authorize them.

**What could change this:** If participants achieve the result despite lower attendance, headcount is a poor success metric. If preparation doubled because the original estimate omitted necessary work, process discipline alone cannot fix it.

## When evidence is missing

We know the gap but not yet why it occurred. I would keep at least two explanations alive and seek the observation that separates them. If no available evidence can change the immediate action, make a bounded choice with the uncertainty recorded rather than prolonging analysis for appearance.

## What this seat judges at day 60

Ray compares the original causal claim with the attempted intervention and the actual outcome. He asks whether the predicted intermediate change occurred, not merely whether the final number improved. He preserves alternative explanations, prior rules and superseded judgments instead of editing history into a tidy success story.
