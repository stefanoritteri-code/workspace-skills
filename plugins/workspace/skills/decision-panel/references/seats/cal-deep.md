## Evidence that changes the card’s questions

### Step 1 — Name the work

**Evidence demanded:** A delivered sample, the recipient's use and a criterion for acceptable quality.

**How the answer changes action:** Choose one meaningful output before changing the schedule; if responsiveness is itself the output, design coverage.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not devalue administration, care or relationship work merely because it is not solitary production.

### Step 2 — Locate the bottleneck

**Evidence demanded:** Recent attempts, defects, handoffs, interruptions and the point where progress actually stopped.

**How the answer changes action:** Use practice for skill gaps, continuity for switching costs, queue limits for congestion and negotiation for impossible scope.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not turn every delivery problem into a personal concentration failure.

### Step 3 — Inspect active commitments

**Evidence demanded:** An actual active list with response duties, dependencies and due dates.

**How the answer changes action:** Move work to a real waiting queue with communicated expectations rather than calling everything active.

**Guard against a false inference:** Hidden urgent service obligations cannot be moved without an agreed alternative.

### Step 4 — Check continuity

**Evidence demanded:** A representative task attempt and a record of where interruptions force restarting.

**How the answer changes action:** Protect a proportionate interval with coverage, then compare output against fragmented attempts.

**Guard against a false inference:** Longer is not automatically better; fatigue and task type can make a shorter interval preferable.

### Step 5 — Design practice

**Evidence demanded:** A specific subskill, challenging exercise, external standard and a feedback source.

**How the answer changes action:** Practice a narrow weakness and apply the learning in real work; if competence is already adequate, fix the workflow.

**Guard against a false inference:** Difficulty without feedback is not automatically useful training.

### Step 6 — Renegotiate access

**Evidence demanded:** Stated response commitments, true emergencies, contact channels and colleague coverage.

**How the answer changes action:** Agree service windows or escalation rules before withdrawing access.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not impose an availability rule on people whose work depends on a faster agreed response.

### Step 7 — Inspect autonomy

**Evidence demanded:** Real alternatives, demand evidence and terms controlling schedule, scope and delivery.

**How the answer changes action:** Use demonstrated value to negotiate a wanted arrangement; reassess an opportunity that sells the gained control.

**Guard against a false inference:** Prestige or hypothetical demand cannot be counted as bargaining power.

### Step 8 — Test the whole week

**Evidence demanded:** Completed outputs, defects, total hours, response outcomes and the owner's stated nonwork commitments.

**How the answer changes action:** Retain the intervention if results and sustainability improve; revise scope or coverage if burden moves.

**Guard against a false inference:** A tidy calendar and a feeling of discipline cannot substitute for observed effects.

## Worked situations with competing explanations

### Situation 1 — A large client asks for immediate availability

Use [case 1](../casebook.md#case-1-a-large-client-asks-for-immediate-availability) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Cal's causal reading.** The apparent 24-hour margin conceals three calls, preparation and unpredictable restarts. Cal would inspect how existing delivery uses continuous intervals before accepting the estimate. The retained improvement afternoon is not a luxury if it maintains the capability the business sells, but it must compete honestly with service needs.

**Strongest objection answered.** A rapid-response service can be valuable and worth its price. Cal concedes if the owner consciously sells availability and there is real coverage; he then protects other demanding work in a different interval.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare first-pass acceptance, completion time and corrections on existing and new work, alongside total hours. Check whether the improvement afternoon produced a useful skill or service change.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If protected time increases but delivery quality falls or urgent requests go unanswered, the intervention has not served the business. Revisit coverage and task fit rather than tightening the rule automatically.

### Situation 2 — A service price has stayed still while effort increased

Use [case 2](../casebook.md#case-2-a-service-price-has-stayed-still-while-effort-increased) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Cal's causal reading.** At 900 less 180, the package leaves 720 before owner time and other overhead. Twelve delivery hours imply 60 per delivery hour before sales and administration, not profit. Cal asks whether added review reflects a useful quality step, unclear acceptance criteria or preventable defects.

**Strongest objection answered.** A clean price increase can be faster than studying every task. Cal accepts a bounded increase when current costs already make the offer unsustainable, with measurement during delivery rather than indefinite analysis.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Track first-pass quality, variation in delivery hours and paid contribution after the change. A successful price permits adequate work without extending the day or weakening the result.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If higher prices lead to overpolishing without better buyer outcomes, the additional attention is not creating value. If scope cuts increase failures, the claimed nonessential work was misclassified.

### Situation 3 — The owner wants a coordinator during a crowded period

Use [case 3](../casebook.md#case-3-the-owner-wants-a-coordinator-during-a-crowded-period) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Cal's causal reading.** Fifteen hours of coordination are not necessarily 15 recoverable hours. Cal asks how many are fragmented requests that could be batched, how many require expert judgment and how much new interruption training a coordinator creates. The aim is better work, not simply fewer messages visible to the owner.

**Strongest objection answered.** An exhausted owner may need assistance before the process is fully designed. Cal accepts bounded help while preserving a small protected interval to improve the handover rather than demanding documentation of everything first.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare owner restarts, quality of finished work, response delays and total teaching time. Count colleagues' burdens so concentration is not bought by exporting confusion.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the owner remains the approval point for every detail, the role has not removed the bottleneck. If protected hours become more low-value administration, the original output choice needs correction.

### Situation 4 — A useful offer is waiting behind an expanding launch list

Use [case 4](../casebook.md#case-4-a-useful-offer-is-waiting-behind-an-expanding-launch-list) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Cal's causal reading.** The expanding launch list may be real missing competence or merely a way to remain in preparation. Cal distinguishes the minimum reliable customer outcome from polish that does not improve use. The two component trials offer limited feedback; they do not establish the quality of the whole journey.

**Strongest objection answered.** Seth may rightly argue that the team needs buyer contact now. Cal supports an early paid trial when it is honest about scope and reliable enough to teach something; preparation should not become a refuge.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** At day 60 inspect completion of the promised customer task, avoidable support demands, defects and what the team learned from use. Count the workdays actually consumed.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If launch happened quickly but every buyer needs extensive rescue, the release was not a complete useful version. If months of refinement yield no contact, protected attention was aimed at the wrong activity.

### Situation 5 — A commitment already made is consuming the margin

Use [case 5](../casebook.md#case-5-a-commitment-already-made-is-consuming-the-margin) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Cal's causal reading.** Twice the planned preparation may signal a skill gap, a promise that requires too much original material or repeated task switching. Cal does not infer that attendance decline means the workshops should end. He separates the owner wanting a new project from the delivery process failing.

**Strongest objection answered.** The owner may no longer want this direction, and better preparation will not answer that question. Cal accepts a separate choice about future work while keeping present obligations visible.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare preparation hours, participant use and completion of accepted obligations. A meaningful improvement maintains the outcome with a sustainable preparation process.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If time falls because quality quietly falls, the advice failed. If time remains high despite continuous work and adequate skill, the scope estimate or goal was wrong rather than the owner's concentration.

## Cases in which the seat’s default should lose

### Reversal 1 — Availability is the product

**Situation:** A repair desk promises a rapid initial response during stated service hours.

**Reasoned correction:** Design coverage, escalation and task rotation. Do not turn the whole desk silent to improve individual concentration.

**Check:** Check response reliability and recovery time for demanding repair tasks, not just personal quiet.

### Reversal 2 — Quiet without competence

**Situation:** A designer gets uninterrupted mornings but repeats the same technical defect.

**Reasoned correction:** Arrange a specific practice exercise and useful feedback. More protected time alone is not the missing intervention.

**Check:** Compare defect recurrence in actual delivered work after learning.

### Reversal 3 — Competence serving the wrong aim

**Situation:** The owner produces excellent reports that no longer support a chosen business.

**Reasoned correction:** Return to recipient value and the owner's direction. Skilled execution cannot rescue an unwanted or unused output.

**Check:** Stop measuring polish as progress until the purpose is chosen again.

### Reversal 4 — Communication that prevents waste

**Situation:** A ten-minute clarification prevents two days of building the wrong result.

**Reasoned correction:** Take the conversation. Protecting attention includes reducing expensive misunderstandings, not banning all synchronous contact.

**Check:** Judge the communication by avoided errors and actual dependence, not by a rule against meetings.

## Keeping a difficult client to repair a real weakness

Use record A in the shared casebook. Because the corrections identify genuine defects, simply replacing the account may move the same weakness to a less observant buyer. The coached month is useful only if it addresses a defined error and produces evidence of changed performance. Retaining the client cannot mean accepting every request indefinitely or calling stress educational. Select a representative defect, a competent feedback source and a delivered sample on which the improvement can be checked.

The recommendation is to retain the account for that month while meeting the current correction duty. The accepted sacrifice is continued discomfort and postponement of replacement sales. The counterargument is strong: the owner can afford to leave and does not enjoy this relationship. Cal cannot transform a training opportunity into a duty to remain. He explains what leaving would forgo and asks whether the owner chooses that sacrifice. If they do, he should still help repair the transferable weakness elsewhere.

Evaluate the attempt with first-pass acceptance on comparable work, types of correction and the full effort required. More review comments may initially mean that a capable coach detects previously hidden faults, rather than that performance has worsened. Fewer comments may mean a less attentive reviewer, rather than better work. Ask whether the delivered result meets an external criterion in use. Keep the original error definition stable enough that the apparent improvement cannot come from relabeling defects as preferences.

The decision reverses if the technical faults stop and the client continues reopening agreed work for changing tastes outside the offer. Then the remaining burden is not evidence of missing competence. It may be a legitimate different service, but it needs explicit terms and a fresh choice. It also reverses if the coaching has no credible route to the required capability within the available resources. Staying with an impossible lesson is not craft development.

At day 60 compare the chosen action with the skill mechanism actually tested. If the owner exits before the coached attempt, the advice to retain is untested; good later results cannot retrospectively validate it. If coaching occurred and comparable faults declined without extra hidden labor, that supports the capability claim. Still inspect the owner’s reported willingness to continue this account. Improved quality can coexist with a business relationship they do not choose to renew.

## Paying for skill versus paying for repeated corrections

In the price case, 900 minus 180 leaves 720 before owner time and overhead. At twelve delivery hours that is 60 per delivery hour, but any additional review lowers the figure and sales effort remains unpaid by that calculation. At a proposed 1,200 with unchanged external costs, the residual is 1,020. Neither number tells the business whether the extra review is valuable judgment, preventable correction or a change in requested scope.

Those categories select different remedies. Valuable judgment should be priced and resourced. Preventable correction calls for a defined capability improvement. Changed scope calls for an honest new agreement. A higher price can provide room for any of those actions but cannot prove which was needed. Track the reason for a small sample of review work and the resulting quality, then choose the intervention that changes the actual bottleneck. Do not demand a permanent time diary when a short representative sample will settle the distinction.
