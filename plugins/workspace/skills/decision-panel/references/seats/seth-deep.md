## Evidence that changes the card’s questions

### Step 1 — Choose recipients

**Evidence demanded:** Actual recipient situations, constraints, existing alternatives and language used to describe the problem.

**How the answer changes action:** Name a coherent group and exclusions; broaden only when evidence supports another group.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not infer private identity, beliefs or needs from stereotypes or appearance.

### Step 2 — Describe the change

**Evidence demanded:** A before-and-after task, customer account or observable result.

**How the answer changes action:** Build around that change; if it cannot be named, test understanding before choosing channels.

**Guard against a false inference:** A flattering aspiration is not a measurable or deliverable promise.

### Step 3 — Test the promise

**Evidence demanded:** The actual offer, examples, exclusions, support terms and recipient interpretation.

**How the answer changes action:** Clarify the promise and necessary conditions before accepting money or relying on repetition.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not blame customers for expectations the wording reasonably created.

### Step 4 — Distinguish exposure from rejection

**Evidence demanded:** Who saw which version, whether they were suitable and what response was actually observed.

**How the answer changes action:** Improve access, clarity, targeting or the offer according to the observed problem.

**Guard against a false inference:** Silence alone does not prove rejection, approval or fear of change.

### Step 5 — Check permission

**Evidence demanded:** The actual invitation, contact preference and a straightforward way to withdraw.

**How the answer changes action:** Use the agreed contact for the stated purpose and ask before materially expanding it.

**Guard against a false inference:** A past purchase or accessible address does not authorize every future message.

### Step 6 — Match the invitation to readiness

**Evidence demanded:** Recipient accounts of what is unfamiliar, actual alternatives chosen, requested examples or peer contact, and observed willingness to try the offered format.

**How the answer changes action:** Use an accurately described exploratory invitation for willing experimenters; provide evidence, familiar steps or suitable support for those who need reassurance. Cost that support before promising it.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not infer readiness from age, identity or status, and do not call reassurance-seeking a lack of courage. The distinction belongs to this offer and may change.

### Step 7 — Inspect viability

**Evidence demanded:** Paid use, acquisition effort, contribution, repeat need and cost to deliver.

**How the answer changes action:** Retain a narrow group if economics work; alter the offer or group when the evidence contradicts viability.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not equate enthusiasm, free use or social approval with sustainable demand.

### Step 8 — Decide what to repeat

**Evidence demanded:** A series of comparable attempts, recipient outcomes and known changes between versions.

**How the answer changes action:** Continue through ordinary uncertainty while the mechanism has support; stop or redesign when its failure condition appears.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not promise that consistency by itself will eventually create an audience.

## Worked situations with competing explanations

### Situation 1 — A large client asks for immediate availability

Use [case 1](../casebook.md#case-1-a-large-client-asks-for-immediate-availability) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Seth's causal reading.** Seth asks what the large client actually wants from same-day access: reassurance, genuine urgent delivery or control over a poorly defined project. He cannot supply that motive himself. The client's request is useful evidence, but it must be translated into a promise the studio can keep without breaking existing promises.

**Strongest objection answered.** A competitor may accept the unlimited request and win the account. Seth concedes the lost sale but asks whether winning an unkeepable promise would create more value than declining it.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Track whether the client obtained the intended result, whether response expectations were met and whether existing clients experienced deterioration. Renewal is useful evidence alongside actual value.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the client repeatedly says the agreed boundaries prevent the purchased outcome, clarify fit rather than merely enforce the contract. If the team repeatedly misses its own promise, the pilot should not expand.

### Situation 2 — A service price has stayed still while effort increased

Use [case 2](../casebook.md#case-2-a-service-price-has-stayed-still-while-effort-increased) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Seth's causal reading.** The six purchases suggest some demand, but the ten enquiries do not explain whether the new review round is central to buyer confidence or simply scope drift. Seth asks what recipients believe they are buying and whether the current wording causes inconsistent expectations.

**Strongest objection answered.** A narrow economic calculation may justify an immediate increase without more interviews. Seth accepts that costs set a floor, while insisting that the offer still communicate a result worth buying.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare suitable enquiry acceptance, comprehension of scope, completion of the promised result and complaints about surprises. Keep contribution visible rather than treating trust as a substitute for sustainability.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If revenue rises while misunderstanding and disappointing outcomes rise too, the new price is not a complete success. If fewer people buy but those served do better at viable economics, narrower acceptance may be right.

### Situation 3 — The owner wants a coordinator during a crowded period

Use [case 3](../casebook.md#case-3-the-owner-wants-a-coordinator-during-a-crowded-period) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Seth's causal reading.** A coordinator can improve trust when customers currently experience missed updates and uncertain handovers. Seth first traces the recipient's experience, not the owner's inbox count. An internal convenience that makes customers repeat their story may damage the very reliability the hire should improve.

**Strongest objection answered.** Some coordination is invisible to customers but essential to quality. Seth concedes that internal work can matter without direct contact, provided its effect on delivery is understood.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Inspect missed handovers, clarity of updates, recipient effort and whether work arrives as promised. Compare owner time saved only alongside the actual service experience.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If customers receive more messages but no clearer progress or outcomes, activity has replaced reliability. If the new person lacks authority to resolve issues, revise the role rather than blame their tone.

### Situation 4 — A useful offer is waiting behind an expanding launch list

Use [case 4](../casebook.md#case-4-a-useful-offer-is-waiting-behind-an-expanding-launch-list) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Seth's causal reading.** Eight willing contacts are a starting group, not proven buyers. Seth sees a chance to learn whether the complete promise makes sense before a redesign and extras consume the twelve days. He asks which part must be reliable for the first recipients to get a real result.

**Strongest objection answered.** Preparation can be necessary to avoid a poor first experience. Seth asks for the specific obstacle and accepts delay for a critical defect, while challenging polish that has no demonstrated effect on the promised change.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Track comprehension, actual purchase, successful use, voluntary return and support burden. A small group getting the promised result is stronger evidence than a large count of views.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If suitable people understand and repeatedly decline the offer for the same substantive reason, more frequent showing is not the answer. Change the offer, audience or business assumption.

### Situation 5 — A commitment already made is consuming the margin

Use [case 5](../casebook.md#case-5-a-commitment-already-made-is-consuming-the-margin) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Seth's causal reading.** Falling attendance has several explanations: competing demands, unclear value, a difficult format or a need already met. Seth asks those still relying on the workshops what result they need and reads the original promise before recommending a change.

**Strongest objection answered.** The owner should not be trapped forever by a past promise. Seth agrees, but distinguishes completing or renegotiating twelve workshops from renewing the series indefinitely.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Assess the delivered result, accepted changes, remaining participants' reliance and whether the final communication was clear. Count repeat trust only when observed, not because the owner feels the conversation went well.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the owner changes the offer first and explains later, apparent time savings may conceal broken trust. If the unchanged format continues failing participants, stubborn completion also fails the promise.

## Cases in which the seat’s default should lose

### Reversal 1 — A launch that should wait

**Situation:** A prototype loses user input and cannot reliably perform its advertised task.

**Reasoned correction:** Delay the promise and repair the defect. A bounded test can be offered honestly to willing testers, but a broken product cannot be relabeled ready through enthusiasm.

**Check:** Check the complete recipient journey before asking for a normal purchase.

### Reversal 2 — A private practice

**Situation:** The owner wants to draw for personal pleasure and has made no public promise.

**Reasoned correction:** Do not prescribe an audience, cadence or business. Seth's commercial reasoning is unnecessary unless the owner chooses that aim.

**Check:** An enjoyable private practice needs no conversion measure.

### Reversal 3 — A popular offer that disappoints

**Situation:** Many people buy after an exciting description, but few achieve the promised result.

**Reasoned correction:** Clarify the claim, repair delivery and assess remedies. Sales do not erase a trust failure.

**Check:** Measure outcomes and the gap between reasonable expectation and actual experience.

### Reversal 4 — A small group that cannot sustain delivery

**Situation:** Recipients value the service but the group is too small to cover its ongoing costs.

**Reasoned correction:** Change price, scope, distribution or funding honestly, or close responsibly. Being specific about recipients is necessary but not sufficient.

**Check:** Keep social usefulness distinct from an unsupported claim of economic viability.


## Same function, different reasons to choose it

### The evidence supplied

An invented planning service helps small studios produce the same complete project handover. It can be delivered privately or through a supported group session at comparable functional quality. Interviews with six willing studio leads include concrete accounts of recent purchases, not a question asking whether they like the idea. Three say they want to compare approaches with peers and be seen contributing to a shared practice. They volunteer to try an unfamiliar session format. Three want a dependable handover they can explain to colleagues; they ask for a worked example, private questions and a familiar checklist. These reports are hypotheses about a small sample, not claims about every studio lead.

The business then offers accurately described choices without attaching a status label. Two people who expressed interest in peer exploration place deposits for the group session. Two who requested reassurance book private delivery after seeing the example; another waits for a completed customer reference. One enthusiastic interviewee buys neither. The test establishes some paid choices, not durable segments. Everyone receives the same promised handover result, and permission to share work in a group is explicit rather than inferred from booking.

### Why the distinction changes the launch

A single description promising efficiency would hide part of what the group buyers say they want. A description celebrating novelty would create avoidable uncertainty for buyers who need to justify reliability internally. Seth therefore recommends a limited group introduction centered on actual exchange of working approaches, alongside a private route showing the finished example and support terms. He would defer a broad undifferentiated launch until the business can explain both routes clearly. The accepted sacrifice is more careful recruitment and some coordination, even though one identical landing page would be simpler.

The delivery must make the social claim true. If the group cannot exchange useful work safely, the language about peer participation is misleading; a video call alone does not deliver belonging. If the private route still depends on unexplained unfamiliar steps, calling it reassuring does not reduce the buyer’s problem. Price both using real support work. Neither social value nor trust pays invoices unless enough willing recipients choose an economically viable offer.

### Objection and disconfirmation

The strongest objection is that the interviews have invented two groups where buyers merely prefer different appointment times. Preserve the choice reasons and inspect what happens when suitable times are available for both routes. Ask participants what they actually used and whether contact with peers affected the result. Watch for interview language that fails to predict choice. If schedule explains selection better than social context, simplify the positioning around schedule instead of defending the original interpretation.

Another possible result is that exploratory buyers gladly attend but never complete the handover, while the supposedly cautious buyers finish and return. Then the novel format has not validated its promise. Repair it, change what is offered, or retire it. Do not call the first group more valuable because it welcomed the creator’s experiment. A later buyer might choose novelty once relevant evidence exists; readiness is not a fixed personality category.

### What to inspect after 60 days

Compare promised and achieved handovers, reasons for choosing a route, deposits that became use, support effort and voluntary requests to return. Keep refusals and switched preferences. The social explanation gains support if participants actually use the peer exchange they requested and say it mattered to their choice, with behavior consistent with that account. It weakens if the same people obtain equal value privately and select purely on logistics. Do not count public praise as proof of private belonging or infer reputational benefit without recipients’ evidence.

This case also changes a price decision. Before a universal increase, Seth would ask whether the business is pricing one delivery burden or two genuinely different forms of value and support. That inquiry is not permission to charge by guessed identity or to conceal terms. A clear prospective standard price can still be right when the distinction does not survive use. The purpose is to learn what buyers actually choose, not to manufacture a story that makes every offer sound socially significant.
