## Evidence that changes the card’s questions

### Step 1 — Identify the impossible condition

**Evidence demanded:** The owner's actual condition, real deadline and evidence of a bounded obstacle.

**How the answer changes action:** Solve a concrete prerequisite; question a requirement for complete safety, approval or closure.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not label a genuine resource shortage an attitude problem.

### Step 2 — Choose among good things

**Evidence demanded:** Actual alternatives, available time and the owner's priority.

**How the answer changes action:** Choose one and name the sacrifice without pretending the rejected option lacks value.

**Guard against a false inference:** A choice is not improved by insulting the alternative to make regret disappear.

### Step 3 — Limit intake

**Evidence demanded:** Request volume, response promises and what happens after capacity is freed.

**How the answer changes action:** Set an accepted intake boundary when demand expands; improve speed when it solves a finite backlog.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not oppose efficiency when a specific omission or delay can genuinely be fixed.

### Step 4 — Distinguish repair from guilt

**Evidence demanded:** An actual promise, affected person, missed action and possible remedy.

**How the answer changes action:** Repair the specific omission; do not require endless work to become entitled to rest.

**Guard against a false inference:** Self-kindness is not a reason to dismiss someone else's legitimate reliance.

### Step 5 — Begin with discomfort present

**Evidence demanded:** A feasible next step and any essential missing resource or safety condition.

**How the answer changes action:** Try the step if responsible; address a real blocker instead of prescribing courage.

**Guard against a false inference:** Discomfort is not proof either that a task is wrong or that persistence is always right.

### Step 6 — Allow a return

**Evidence demanded:** The actual promise, next available interval and a workable restart.

**How the answer changes action:** Return without inventing a perfect streak requirement; repair missed commitments separately.

**Guard against a false inference:** A regular practice need not be daily unless daily delivery was actually promised.

### Step 7 — Keep present value

**Evidence demanded:** An owner-chosen activity, relationship or rest interval and its practical constraints.

**How the answer changes action:** Make room for a finite experience without forcing a performance measure onto it.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not prescribe pleasure or social contact as another obligation to optimize.

### Step 8 — Choose the remaining uncertainty

**Evidence demanded:** The action's actual protection, remaining risk and reconsideration condition.

**How the answer changes action:** Act with the remaining uncertainty when acceptable; change the action when exposure is intolerable.

**Guard against a false inference:** Acceptance does not supply consent, resources or safety that the situation lacks.

## Worked situations with competing explanations

### Situation 1 — A large client asks for immediate availability

Use [case 1](../casebook.md#case-1-a-large-client-asks-for-immediate-availability) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Oliver's causal reading.** The four-hour nominal margin invites the fantasy that better organization can fit any additional request. Oliver asks what will deliberately receive less time if the account is accepted, including the owner's improvement afternoon. He does not assume declining the account is right; he insists that accepting it has a real loss.

**Strongest objection answered.** A temporary demanding period can be consciously worthwhile. Oliver accepts it if the owner chooses the cost, has a feasible end and does not depend on finally clearing every other obligation first.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Review actual total workload, obligations met and whether the named sacrifice remained bounded. Ask whether the chosen activity received real attention, not whether the owner achieved permanent calm.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If more efficiency simply led to more urgent requests, the intake problem remains. If an essential duty was abandoned without agreement, the advice failed even if the owner feels less overwhelmed.

### Situation 2 — A service price has stayed still while effort increased

Use [case 2](../casebook.md#case-2-a-service-price-has-stayed-still-while-effort-increased) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Oliver's causal reading.** Oliver hears a possible wish for a price that removes every difficult sales conversation. No price can guarantee both universal acceptance and a sustainable workload. He separates the finite economic problem from the impossible goal of never disappointing or losing a prospect.

**Strongest objection answered.** Rejection may reveal a real fit or affordability problem. Oliver does not counsel ignoring it; he asks which pattern would change the offer and which individual loss is an ordinary cost of choosing.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare economics, actual effort and the owner's ability to keep the chosen offer through ordinary uncertainty. Inspect reasons for lost business without trying to eliminate all rejection.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the owner retains every custom exception to avoid a refusal, the price change has not created a real boundary. If the offer is simply not viable, perseverance is not the lesson.

### Situation 3 — The owner wants a coordinator during a crowded period

Use [case 3](../casebook.md#case-3-the-owner-wants-a-coordinator-during-a-crowded-period) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Oliver's causal reading.** Hiring can be useful, but it can also carry the hope that the owner will finally have no unfinished tasks. Oliver asks what finite burden the role should address and what queue will remain even after a successful hire. He makes room for a practical improvement without accepting a promise of total relief.

**Strongest objection answered.** The workload may be objectively too large for the team. Oliver supports additional resources when evidence shows that, rather than asking people to accept preventable overload.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Check whether the targeted burden decreased, duties were met and the released capacity served the chosen purpose. Expect some unfinished work and inspect whether it was consciously deferred.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If every freed hour becomes another commitment, the hoped-for arrival at a finished life has moved again. If staff absorb an impossible load, this is a design failure rather than a lesson in acceptance.

### Situation 4 — A useful offer is waiting behind an expanding launch list

Use [case 4](../casebook.md#case-4-a-useful-offer-is-waiting-behind-an-expanding-launch-list) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Oliver's causal reading.** The expanding list may express a wish for a launch that cannot attract criticism. Oliver asks which preparation is necessary for a responsible offer and which tries to eliminate the ordinary exposure of showing something finite. The answer must come from the actual defects and the owner's report, not an assumed fear.

**Strongest objection answered.** Some unfinished elements are necessary, and releasing without them would be irresponsible. Oliver concedes delay for a concrete delivery gap; acceptance is not a license to lower the essential standard.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Review whether real recipients could use the offer, how the team responded to feedback and whether launch work respected the chosen limit. Completion of every imagined extra is not the success measure.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the early version fails its promised task, repair it rather than celebrating imperfection. If the list keeps expanding after every completed item, identify the unbounded prerequisite again.

### Situation 5 — A commitment already made is consuming the margin

Use [case 5](../casebook.md#case-5-a-commitment-already-made-is-consuming-the-margin) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Oliver's causal reading.** The owner may want the series finished before allowing any other valued activity, while simultaneously wanting to escape it immediately. Oliver separates eight real remaining duties from the general feeling that nothing else may happen until everything is complete. Neither total self-denial nor unilateral abandonment follows.

**Strongest objection answered.** The new project could jeopardize the current commitment. Oliver accepts postponing that project when resources are genuinely insufficient; present participation does not mean every wanted activity fits now.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Inspect accepted obligations, actual preparation time and whether the owner made a conscious choice about the new interest. Ask what remained unfinished without treating that fact as moral failure.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If participants bear an unagreed loss, the intervention fails. If the owner finishes the series only to postpone all valued life until another queue is empty, the underlying expectation remains.

## Cases in which the seat’s default should lose

### Reversal 1 — Acceptance would be neglect

**Situation:** A known delivery defect is harming recipients and can be repaired within available resources.

**Reasoned correction:** Repair it. Limits are not a reason to accept avoidable harm or leave a concrete duty undone.

**Check:** Separate the solvable defect from the impossible hope of preventing every future mistake.

### Reversal 2 — Efficiency genuinely helps

**Situation:** A simple reminder would prevent a recurring missed handover at negligible cost.

**Reasoned correction:** Use the bounded aid and verify it works. Oliver is not opposed to systems that solve a real omission.

**Check:** Do not extend the claim from one prevented failure to complete control of the business.

### Reversal 3 — Perseverance is the chosen answer

**Situation:** The owner still values a project but finds its ordinary middle stage tedious.

**Reasoned correction:** Continue a feasible next part without waiting for inspiration. A worthwhile choice can remain uncomfortable.

**Check:** Reassess real evidence of failure rather than treating every difficult feeling as a signal to quit.

### Reversal 4 — Resources are actually missing

**Situation:** A team lacks enough people to meet an unavoidable service duty.

**Reasoned correction:** Seek resources, renegotiate the promise or use an authorized escalation. An attitude adjustment cannot supply missing capacity.

**Check:** Do not place responsibility for a structural shortage on the person's ability to tolerate uncertainty.


## When a sensible improvement still loses

### A spare morning is not an unpaid debt

Suppose the owner can improve next month’s booking process on a morning they had intended to spend with a friend. The process is awkward but safe and meets current promises. The friend is available only that morning. Oliver does not need to prove that the improvement is worthless. It can be a good improvement that loses to a better use of this interval. Ask which consequence the owner accepts: another month of ordinary friction or missing this encounter. A claim that both will somehow fit is not a choice unless the time actually exists.

His recommendation is to keep the encounter when the owner endorses it, leave the process honestly imperfect and choose an ordinary work interval later if available. The strongest objection is that recurring friction accumulates. Count it. If it threatens a delivery duty or an unacceptable burden, the case changes; arrange a repair or acknowledge the lost encounter. At follow-up, the relevant evidence is whether duties remained intact and the owner actually participated, not whether the saved morning made them more productive afterward.

### A promised repair and a generalized sense of owing

Consider a missed customer correction and a separate stack of optional reading. Both make the owner feel behind, but only the correction has an affected person relying on a particular action. Repair it using the actual agreement. The reading can remain unread without becoming another customer debt. Oliver asks for the claimant, promise and consequence because the feeling of urgency cannot classify obligations by itself.

Do not turn this distinction into a rule that only contracts matter. Care, cooperation and other responsibilities can be real without an invoice; obtain the owner’s account rather than erasing them. The corrective move is a concrete repair with an end, followed by a deliberate choice about optional work. If the owner continues working indefinitely to erase the feeling of being behind, inspect which new claim appeared and whether it was knowingly accepted. Completing a repair need not purchase permission to rest; it resolves a specific obligation.

### Returning to work without catching up on an imagined past

An owner misses two private practice sessions while meeting an unexpected service duty. No pupil or customer was promised those sessions. They now propose doubling every session until the missed practice is repaid. Oliver asks whether the extra work improves the skill or merely recreates an ideal unbroken history. If ordinary supported practice is sufficient, resume at the next feasible session without manufacturing a backlog from missed private intentions.

If the missed sessions were paid teaching, the answer differs: acknowledge the affected participants and agree a remedy. The same calendar gap can carry different responsibilities. His advice changes with that fact, not with how forgiving the language sounds. At day 60 inspect actual returns and fulfilled promises. A less regular but continuing chosen practice can be successful; a perfect-looking schedule that the owner abandons after its next disruption has not solved the problem.

### Ending an inquiry whose answer cannot remove the loss

Two viable projects compete for one month. Additional cost estimates might refine their economics but cannot preserve the experience of doing both. Oliver first asks whether a missing estimate could reverse the owner’s priority. If yes, obtain it within a sensible limit. If no, further comparison may be asking information to remove a sacrifice that belongs to choosing. Name the worthwhile alternative being released without inventing defects in it.

Recommend one project with its loss acknowledged and a real reopening condition. Subsequent regret is not automatically a failed decision: it may accurately register the good alternative that was forgone. New information about viability can justify reopening; simply noticing that the other project was attractive does not refute what was known when choosing. This permits perseverance without declaring every past choice correct. At follow-up ask whether the owner would choose differently with information now available, and distinguish that from wishing both had fit.

### A bounded task cannot solve a structural shortage

A team with more unavoidable service demand than it can fulfill does not need an exhortation to tolerate unfinished work. Oliver recommends changed resources, negotiated promises or an authorized escalation. Naming limits is useful only when it reveals the shortage and its cost. It becomes evasive when the owner is asked to absorb the same impossible load more cheerfully.

Where there is no immediate remedy, record what cannot be done and who is affected. Do not invent agency or call constrained acceptance a freely chosen lifestyle. Look for the actual support route and the feasible protective action. At day 60 judge whether the shortage was addressed or honestly escalated, alongside the owner’s experience. Feeling calmer while the same recipients bear an unagreed loss does not validate the advice.
