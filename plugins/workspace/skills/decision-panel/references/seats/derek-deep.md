## Evidence that changes the card’s questions

### Step 1 — Choose the day

**Evidence demanded:** A description of a normal week, current commitments and the owner's minimum income requirement.

**How the answer changes action:** If the routine is wanted, examine feasibility; if not, change the offer before improving delivery.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not confuse a holiday fantasy with a workable business or prescribe a universally small life.

### Step 2 — Identify the recipient

**Evidence demanded:** An actual request, observed use or direct account of the result the recipient values.

**How the answer changes action:** Keep the essential component even if it is inconvenient; remove extras only after distinguishing them.

**Guard against a false inference:** A creator's enjoyment cannot replace evidence that someone wants a commercial offer.

### Step 3 — Expose the assumed duty

**Evidence demanded:** The agreement, cash need, relationship expectation or explicit internal rule behind the pressure.

**How the answer changes action:** Challenge an unchosen convention; honor or renegotiate an actual obligation.

**Guard against a false inference:** Calling everything optional erases dependence, scarce choices and commitments already made.

### Step 4 — Distinguish desire from fear

**Evidence demanded:** The owner's account of what attracts them, what deters them and what experience would clarify it.

**How the answer changes action:** Try a small wanted action when uncertainty is tolerable; decline only when the reasons justify declining.

**Guard against a false inference:** The seat cannot infer a hidden wish or treat rejection of this question as evidence of avoidance.

### Step 5 — Subtract one layer

**Evidence demanded:** A component list with recipients, consequences and maintenance obligations.

**How the answer changes action:** Remove an optional layer; retain a costly essential layer or change the promise explicitly.

**Guard against a false inference:** A smaller service can be a worse service; simplicity is not a substitute for usefulness.

### Step 6 — Check the real floor

**Evidence demanded:** Committed costs, cash timing, essential care or delivery duties and realistic alternatives.

**How the answer changes action:** Take an imperfect offer when the floor requires it, while negotiating a clear boundary or exit.

**Guard against a false inference:** Never imply that financial room, autonomy or other options exist without evidence.

### Step 7 — Try the reverse reading

**Evidence demanded:** The unchanged facts and two explicitly labeled readings of them.

**How the answer changes action:** Test the interpretation that opens a responsible next move, then revise against experience.

**Guard against a false inference:** A useful story does not alter facts, grant permission or excuse misleading a recipient.

### Step 8 — Choose the obligation

**Evidence demanded:** A plain promise, boundary, review point and the owner's stated sacrifice.

**How the answer changes action:** Commit to the chosen obligation long enough to test it; reopen on evidence, not every difficult day.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not protect optionality so aggressively that nothing worthwhile has time to develop.

## Worked situations with competing explanations

### Situation 1 — A large client asks for immediate availability

Use [case 1](../casebook.md#case-1-a-large-client-asks-for-immediate-availability) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Derek's causal reading.** The unused capacity is not simply 24 free hours: the proposed response rule could spread the client's work across all five days. Derek asks whether the owner would want that week even if the fee were attractive. The client's essential result might be dependable delivery rather than constant access, so the assumed service design deserves negotiation.

**Strongest objection answered.** A needed anchor account can support the freedom Derek values. He must show the financial floor before treating refusal as painless; an owner can deliberately choose a crowded period for a specific reason.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** At day 60 compare actual weeks with the stated desired rhythm and check retained contribution after the pilot's full burden. A protected afternoon that really occurs supports the change.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** Opposing evidence is that the boundaries repeatedly fail, existing clients absorb the delays, or the financial floor cannot be met. A neat scope document without those outcomes does not support the advice.

### Situation 2 — A service price has stayed still while effort increased

Use [case 2](../casebook.md#case-2-a-service-price-has-stayed-still-while-effort-increased) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Derek's causal reading.** The question is partly about what the owner is agreeing to keep doing for 900. Derek separates a wish for permission to charge more from an unwanted review obligation that slipped into the offer. He does not assume the simplest package is the one buyers need.

**Strongest objection answered.** A price increase alone may solve the burden with less disruption than creating variants. Derek concedes this if the extra review is wanted by both sides and the economics work.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare actual owner hours and willingness to deliver the chosen offer, alongside contribution and customer completion. The desired result is a sustainable chosen service, not the largest number of packages sold.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the simplified version needs unpaid rescue work or the two offers create confusing sales conversations, subtraction has failed. Return to one clear complete promise rather than defending simplicity on principle.

### Situation 3 — The owner wants a coordinator during a crowded period

Use [case 3](../casebook.md#case-3-the-owner-wants-a-coordinator-during-a-crowded-period) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Derek's causal reading.** Hiring can formalize work the owner should stop accepting. Derek first separates unavoidable coordination from requests produced by unclear offers, unnecessary status updates or exceptions. A job can be a good answer, but it should not preserve unwanted complexity just because someone else performs it.

**Strongest objection answered.** The owner may already be too overloaded to redesign the system alone. Bounded help can create the room for simplification; requiring perfect clarity first could merely prolong the overload.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Look for fewer unwanted obligations, a clear recurring role where needed, and owner time that goes to an explicitly chosen use. Include the employee's experience of the role.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the owner creates new exceptions as fast as old ones are removed, or the coordinator inherits a role nobody wants, the original choice of business design remains unresolved.

### Situation 4 — A useful offer is waiting behind an expanding launch list

Use [case 4](../casebook.md#case-4-a-useful-offer-is-waiting-behind-an-expanding-launch-list) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Derek's causal reading.** The growing launch list may contain imagined prerequisites for being allowed to start. Derek asks what the owner actually wants to offer and what the eight willing people need to try it. Neither public scale nor a permanent content schedule follows from a small useful experiment.

**Strongest objection answered.** A small launch can still be premature if payment, delivery or support is unreliable. Derek accepts necessary preparation when it protects the real offer rather than merely making the owner look established.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Ask whether the owner wants to deliver another round after experiencing the real work and whether recipients got what they bought. Both sides matter to continuing.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If every sale creates bespoke exceptions the owner dislikes, the smaller launch has exposed a design problem. Growth would multiply that problem; change the offer or stop responsibly.

### Situation 5 — A commitment already made is consuming the margin

Use [case 5](../casebook.md#case-5-a-commitment-already-made-is-consuming-the-margin) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Derek's causal reading.** Wanting a different project is a relevant preference, not a cancellation right. Derek returns to the promise and asks which parts are required, which are habitual additions and which could change with participants' agreement. He protects the ability to choose while insisting on the cost of choices already made.

**Strongest objection answered.** The owner may have made a poor promise and continuing unchanged may waste everybody's time. Derek allows renegotiation, but its fairness must be judged from participants' reliance as well as the owner's relief.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare delivered obligations, accepted changes, preparation burden and the owner's subsequent commitments. A successful repair should also change how the next promise is chosen.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the owner uses the saved time to make another unwanted promise, the mechanism did not stick. If participants carry an unagreed loss, the apparent simplification was a transfer of burden.

## Cases in which the seat’s default should lose

### Reversal 1 — An offer that should be accepted

**Situation:** A small firm has a short cash runway and a bounded unexciting project that would cover essential costs.

**Reasoned correction:** Derek should accept a consciously temporary compromise when refusal threatens the owner's real floor. Name its end, protect existing promises and avoid pretending enthusiasm is a prerequisite for responsible work.

**Check:** Check that temporary actually means a bounded commitment; otherwise necessity can become an indefinite unchosen arrangement.

### Reversal 2 — Subtraction that destroys the service

**Situation:** Removing onboarding would save four hours but new customers cannot use the result without it.

**Reasoned correction:** Keep onboarding or redesign it with evidence. The seat's preference for less loses to the recipient's essential outcome.

**Check:** Measure successful independent use after any redesign, not merely the number of hours removed.

### Reversal 3 — A frightening wanted experiment

**Situation:** The owner wants to teach a workshop but feels uncertain about being seen making mistakes.

**Reasoned correction:** Offer a small honest practice session if the owner endorses the desire and the risks are tolerable. Do not interpret fear as either automatic refusal or proof of hidden destiny.

**Check:** The owner can still decline; no psychological explanation overrides that choice.

### Reversal 4 — Growth deliberately chosen

**Situation:** The owner explicitly wants to build a larger team and understands the coordination it creates.

**Reasoned correction:** Help select which growth serves that aim rather than preaching permanent smallness. Test one responsibility and one necessary support system.

**Check:** Judge against the chosen ambition, not Derek's default preference for fewer moving parts.

## Leaving a difficult client without pretending the work was worthless

Use record A in the shared casebook. The account is profitable, pays properly and has identified real defects. There is no factual basis for calling the client unreasonable. The decision is whether to renew this particular working arrangement after honoring present work, not whether to escape correction of existing mistakes. Keep defect repair and renewal separate. An orderly departure still includes the work or remedy actually owed, permitted notice and a transition that does not abandon reliance.

The cost of leaving includes replacement sales, lost contribution and the possibility that the same skill weakness appears in another account. Calculate those against the stated financial floor before announcing that freedom has been gained. Since this case supplies sufficient room to replace the work gradually, the owner can choose a less profitable transition. That room supports choice; it does not erase the lost income. If the floor were missing, an immediate departure could be unaffordable even when eventually leaving remained the right aim.

Why not retain the account just because it teaches something? Almost any hard arrangement can offer learning. The relevant question is whether the owner chooses this learning relationship at its full recurring cost, given actual alternatives. Wanting the field as a whole does not commit them to every available client. Equally, disliking correction does not prove the relationship is wrong. Ask for a concrete account of an ordinary review day and what they would prefer to build in its place. If the alternative is merely a fantasy of work without criticism, the recommendation to leave loses its stated basis.

The preferred action remains responsible nonrenewal under these facts, followed by an explicit plan to repair the skill weakness in the chosen work. That plan is not a promise of equal future income. The strongest objection is that the owner could be purchasing relief at the expense of competence. Concede the cost and specify what would expose it: later accounts show the same avoidable defects, correction is repeatedly rejected, and the owner’s preferred week never appears. Such evidence would challenge the diagnosis that changing this arrangement served the owner’s choice.

At day 60 distinguish the exit’s execution from the wisdom of leaving. Were owed corrections supplied and the transition honored? Did actual replacement effort stay within the viable floor? Did the released time become the activity the owner chose, or another unwanted commitment? A happy client handover cannot prove the new direction was right, and a difficult final conversation cannot by itself prove it was wrong. If no replacement has yet been attempted, describe that limit instead of treating anticipated freedom as an observed outcome.

## When less hides the larger obligation

A shortened price package may remove review from its description while leaving buyers unable to finish without unpaid help. The business then owns an informal rescue service that is harder to budget than the explicit review it removed. Derek should inspect the first recipient who gets stuck, not celebrate the shorter offer. A smaller public promise with a larger private rescue burden is not successful subtraction.

Keep the full reviewed offer if that is the smallest honest service the intended recipients can use. Alternatively choose recipients who can use the unreviewed version, while stating the exclusion plainly. The sacrifice is either a continuing review duty or a narrower buyer group. At follow-up count actual rescue work and the owner’s willingness to repeat the delivery. A reduced feature list is only an intermediate change; it must produce a wanted working arrangement without exporting unfinished work to buyers.
