## Evidence that changes the card’s questions

### A recipient cannot finish

Read one failed or incomplete delivery from the recipient’s side: what they received, what they attempted, where they stopped and what the business still owes. A list of features is weak evidence because it does not show whether those parts connect. Jason — product asks for a demonstration of completion or a credible description of the manual help required. A frequently requested addition may be cosmetic; an obscure handover may be indispensable. Frequency alone cannot decide which to keep.

If the remaining work is optional to the agreed result, remove or defer it openly. If the missing part is essential, the choice moves to repair, staffing, timing or renegotiation. A promise is not made feasible by renaming its failed remainder an extra. This is why recipient consequence precedes an internal effort limit. A shorter plan that strands every buyer is operationally worse than a longer honest one.

### The repair has no empowered owner

Inspect a recent exception: who noticed it, who knew what to do, who could approve the cost and who actually answered the recipient. Separate these people when the evidence separates them. If the capable person repeatedly waits for approval, David — ownership recommends moving an appropriately bounded decision or changing the promise. If nobody has the necessary competence, a wider approval allowance merely permits faster mistakes.

Authority must include a path for cases outside it. A coordinator allowed to settle routine changes but unable to reach anyone for an unusual defect is not operating a complete service. Test a handover using a realistic exception before announcing the role is independent. Good documentation is evidence only after another supported person can use it; page count does not measure transferability.

### Ordinary variation breaks the average

Compare demand over complete jobs, including preparation, correction and follow-up. A quiet delivery day followed by a heavy support day is one service burden, even when different people perform it. Where work is too new for a sample, use a range from genuinely comparable work and state what makes the comparison weak. Do not translate willingness to spend a certain amount of time into evidence that the work takes that long.

Protection follows the likely failure. If requests arrive unpredictably, coverage and intake rules may matter more than uninterrupted production. If corrections cluster after a customer event, plan help around that event. If fatigue has accumulated, reduce exposure and provide actual recovery before adding sales. There is no standard block of work followed by a standard pause; the job’s support profile decides where capacity must remain available.

### A convenient cut removes the reason to buy

Jason — product can reject a proposed reduction even when it would make the operation easier. For example, removing personal setup might save half the service time while leaving new customers unable to begin. The alternative is to retain setup, improve its delivery, change the intended recipients, or admit that the current price and timing cannot support the promise. Those are materially different offers, not verbal ways to make the same plan fit.

David — ownership can meanwhile reject a useful product arrangement because only an exhausted owner can deliver it. The registers do not average their objections into a compromise that satisfies neither. They identify which feasible change addresses both: trained cover, a more suitable supplier, slower intake or an accepted different promise. If none does, recommend declining the new work while remedying existing obligations.

### Control survives a departure

Possession of a tool may reduce one dependency and create another on its sole maintainer. Examine permitted portability, documented operations, practical replacement effort and the consequences of temporary failure. Buying a well-supported service may give more usable control than hosting something nobody can repair. Conversely, a cheap external service can be expensive when it blocks a necessary handover or change.

Do not argue from self-sufficiency. Ask which future decision the business needs to retain and what realistic arrangement preserves it. A backup copy without a person capable of restoring it is incomplete protection; a transferable manual workaround may be sufficient for a modest service. The decision should change if the alleged dependency risk cannot affect any needed action or if its remedy consumes more operating attention than the service can sustain.

### Repair remains after a sale

Keep an estimate of continuing work beside the production estimate, without assuming both have the same timing. Include the person who will handle help, the cost of a failed delivery and the route for ending or transferring the service. A sale with a brief production task can still create an enduring response obligation. Declining further intake may be justified even when the next artifact is quick to produce.

At follow-up distinguish a useful delivery from a sustainable operation. Product can have been right about what buyers needed while ownership was wrong about upkeep. Preserve both results in the single history. A service that met its promise through unplanned nights has not validated the operating design; a well-controlled service nobody could use has not validated the product choice.

## Worked situations with competing explanations

### Situation 1 — A large client asks for immediate availability

Use [case 1](../casebook.md#case-1-a-large-client-asks-for-immediate-availability) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Jason and David's causal reading.** Jason — product sees a plausible useful account but asks whether same-day changes belong to the essential outcome. David — ownership sees an access promise that could give one client practical control over the studio's schedule. The 20-hour estimate excludes several visible claims on the remaining margin.

**Strongest objection answered.** An anchor customer can sustain the business and deserve a tailored service. The registers can agree to an exception when its cost, duration and authority are explicit rather than silently becoming the new default.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Product checks useful delivery and client comprehension. Ownership checks actual control of scope, interruption load, total hours and dependence on the account. Record both forecasts in the same seat history.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If delivery succeeds only through nights or another client's delay, the operation was not sustainable. If boundaries are honored but the result is not useful, the product minimum was wrong.

### Situation 2 — A service price has stayed still while effort increased

Use [case 2](../casebook.md#case-2-a-service-price-has-stayed-still-while-effort-increased) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Jason and David's causal reading.** Jason — product asks whether the extra review is essential to a complete result. David — ownership asks who keeps granting it and whether the original price ever covered the actual promise. A price debate cannot be solved by requiring staff to absorb expanding scope within the same hours.

**Strongest objection answered.** Two variants might serve different buyers well, but they also create sales and operational complexity. Jason and David permit them only when the distinction reflects a real need and is simple to deliver.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Product checks recipient completion and fewer scope surprises. Ownership checks contribution, actual work hours and whether the person responsible can enforce the agreed boundary.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If staff remain unable to refuse extras, the package definition has not changed the operation. If cuts make the result unusable, pricing clarity cannot redeem the product loss.

### Situation 3 — The owner wants a coordinator during a crowded period

Use [case 3](../casebook.md#case-3-the-owner-wants-a-coordinator-during-a-crowded-period) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Jason and David's causal reading.** Jason — product asks what recurring work the coordinator would make better. David — ownership asks which decisions the role can actually own. Hiring a person to relay requests through an unchanged approval bottleneck can add cost and meetings without improving delivery.

**Strongest objection answered.** The owner may need relief immediately. Jason and David allow supported temporary help if available and honestly scoped, while refusing to call an undefined collection of tasks a durable role.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Product checks handover reliability and useful delivery. Ownership checks decision autonomy, support burden, total hours and whether the added role made the business easier to operate.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the team now spends more time explaining work than delivering it, examine role design and timing. A capable hire should not be blamed for an incoherent job.

### Situation 4 — A useful offer is waiting behind an expanding launch list

Use [case 4](../casebook.md#case-4-a-useful-offer-is-waiting-behind-an-expanding-launch-list) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Jason and David's causal reading.** Jason — product identifies the complete buyer journey rather than a list of attractive launch features. David — ownership asks who will support the first buyers and whether those hours are included in the twelve available days. A redesign, video, library and automation cannot all be treated as fixed requirements without an evidence-based estimate or more resources.

**Strongest objection answered.** Some presentation work may be necessary for trust or comprehension. Jason and David keep it when a real recipient obstacle is shown, and reject the assumption that every aesthetic preference is essential.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Product checks successful use and the quality of essentials. Ownership checks actual time spent, scope changes, support load and whether named support cover handled the actual post-sale demand.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the launch meets the date only through hidden overtime, the plan failed its budget. If optional cuts remove a necessary part of the journey, the minimum was incorrectly defined.

### Situation 5 — A commitment already made is consuming the margin

Use [case 5](../casebook.md#case-5-a-commitment-already-made-is-consuming-the-margin) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Jason and David's causal reading.** Jason — product inspects what participants need from the remaining eight sessions. David — ownership asks whether preparation expectations expanded without anyone having authority to cut. The owner wanting a new project does not settle either the useful minimum or the outstanding promise.

**Strongest objection answered.** Participants may have relied on the original format, so an internal time limit cannot decide a change. Jason and David accept a larger temporary effort or a fair remedy when the minimum promise cannot be reduced.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Product checks the promised participant outcome. Ownership checks preparation hours, accepted changes, recovery and whether the next commitment uses a realistic scope budget.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If remaining support has no responsible operator after the workshops, the service was treated as finished before its obligations ended. If the smaller format weakens the result, a calmer calendar does not validate it.

## Cases in which the seat’s default should lose

### Reversal 1 — Scope cannot move

**Situation:** A fixed external obligation contains an essential result that exceeds the available interval.

**Reasoned correction:** Renegotiate time, resources or the promise through the authorized route. Do not impose a time budget that can be met only by hidden overtime or non-delivery.

**Check:** Distinguish real constraints from negotiable preferences and preserve the customer's reliance.

### Reversal 2 — Incoming service is not a project

**Situation:** A support team handles unpredictable requests throughout the day.

**Reasoned correction:** Design service coverage and intake limits suited to that work. Do not force every request into a multiweek project rhythm.

**Check:** Check actual response outcomes and workload rather than compliance with an arbitrary project timetable.

### Reversal 3 — Ownership is too costly

**Situation:** Maintaining an internal replacement would consume the only person able to improve the core offer.

**Reasoned correction:** Use a suitable supplier with realistic portability or exit planning. Ownership has value only after its operating cost is counted.

**Check:** Inspect the dependency's actual risk, not a general preference for independence.

### Reversal 4 — Calm conceals a problem

**Situation:** A team stops raising defects because criticism is treated as disruption.

**Reasoned correction:** Restore a clear route for reporting and fixing problems. Calm cannot mean suppressing evidence or asking customers to tolerate preventable failure.

**Check:** Judge the operation by honest useful delivery and sustainable work, not the absence of disagreement.
