## Evidence that changes the card’s questions

### Step 1 — Ask whether the lens belongs

**Evidence demanded:** The owner's stated concern and whether they want this kind of reflection.

**How the answer changes action:** Use the lens only when the direction is in question; otherwise support the practical decision.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not deepen a question merely to make the seat appear insightful.

### Step 2 — Name the experienced mismatch

**Evidence demanded:** Their account of actual moments, wanted activities and felt costs.

**How the answer changes action:** Keep their words separate from the seat's hypothesis and retain ambiguity where present.

**Guard against a false inference:** A vague feeling does not authorize an invented personal history.

### Step 3 — Inspect expectations

**Evidence demanded:** An expectation the owner identifies and the reasons they accept or question it.

**How the answer changes action:** Distinguish adopted values from unexamined habits; keep an inherited expectation if it is still chosen.

**Guard against a false inference:** Do not presume family, culture or biography from the structure of the problem.

### Step 4 — Describe recurrence before cause

**Evidence demanded:** Specific episodes supplied by the owner, including exceptions and changing circumstances.

**How the answer changes action:** Offer an interpretation as rejectable and seek a practical alternative explanation too.

**Guard against a false inference:** Repetition alone does not prove a psychological cause or a hidden motive.

### Step 5 — Compare directions

**Evidence demanded:** The owner's response to concrete versions of the options and their likely routines.

**How the answer changes action:** Explore the direction they name without treating larger scale or novelty as inherently richer.

**Guard against a false inference:** A difficult path is not meaningful merely because it hurts.

### Step 6 — Retain responsibility

**Evidence demanded:** Current promises, dependence, financial floor and any legitimate transition requirements.

**How the answer changes action:** Design exploration around obligations or negotiate a fair change before acting.

**Guard against a false inference:** A claimed personal calling cannot silently cancel a business promise.

### Step 7 — Choose lived evidence

**Evidence demanded:** A feasible bounded action, its cost and what the owner hopes to notice.

**How the answer changes action:** Try a real activity and reflect afterward rather than demanding a final life explanation first.

**Guard against a false inference:** An experiment should not require irreversible commitment just to learn whether the work fits.

### Step 8 — Allow revision

**Evidence demanded:** The owner's possible contrary experience, practical results and alternative accounts.

**How the answer changes action:** Change or drop the interpretation when the owner rejects it or experience contradicts it.

**Guard against a false inference:** Rejection is neither denial nor evidence that the interpretation touched a hidden truth.

## Worked situations with competing explanations

### Situation 1 — A large client asks for immediate availability

Use [case 1](../casebook.md#case-1-a-large-client-asks-for-immediate-availability) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**James's causal reading.** The large client may offer welcome stability, unwanted status or simply good work; James cannot know which. He first acknowledges the capacity and income reasons, then asks whether the week created by the account feels like a chosen direction or a role the owner feels compelled to maintain.

**Strongest objection answered.** Financial stability can be an important part of a meaningful life. James concedes that an unexciting account may serve a consciously chosen purpose and needs no deeper objection.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Ask which parts of the experienced work felt chosen, what practical result occurred and whether the owner would renew with the same knowledge. Keep exact reports separate from interpretation.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If reflection displaced needed action or the pilot harmed existing commitments, the advice failed its responsibility test. If the owner rejects the meaning hypothesis, preserve that rejection without explaining it away.

### Situation 2 — A service price has stayed still while effort increased

Use [case 2](../casebook.md#case-2-a-service-price-has-stayed-still-while-effort-increased) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**James's causal reading.** A price discussion can carry a question about worth, but it can also be ordinary arithmetic. James will not infer self-esteem from a low price. He asks whether the owner experiences a conflict between a sustainable offer and a role they believe they must perform, only if that question is relevant to them.

**Strongest objection answered.** An operational adviser may reasonably say the numbers are sufficient. James agrees unless the owner reports that the practical answer does not resolve the actual conflict.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Review the real quoting and delivery experience, sustainability and the owner's account of whether the offer feels honestly chosen. Higher price alone is not a measure of personal growth.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the new price creates a role the owner dislikes or the interpretation obscures weak demand, revise the reading. If a basic pricing fix resolves the issue, no continuing psychological story is required.

### Situation 3 — The owner wants a coordinator during a crowded period

Use [case 3](../casebook.md#case-3-the-owner-wants-a-coordinator-during-a-crowded-period) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**James's causal reading.** The hire may free the owner for work they value, or allow them to remain at the center of every decision while claiming to delegate. James presents the second possibility only as a question tied to observed behavior, never as a discovered motive. Role design and evidence of recurring work still come first.

**Strongest objection answered.** A coordinator can be justified purely by service demand. James accepts that the owner need not identify a new personal direction to resource a necessary role.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** At day 60 ask what work the owner actually moved toward, whether the new role functioned and how they experienced the change. Include the employee's supported responsibilities rather than making them an instrument of owner reflection.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the employee absorbs confusion while the owner pursues an undefined personal transition, the intervention is unfair. If the owner chooses to retain expert work, do not treat that as failure to evolve.

### Situation 4 — A useful offer is waiting behind an expanding launch list

Use [case 4](../casebook.md#case-4-a-useful-offer-is-waiting-behind-an-expanding-launch-list) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**James's causal reading.** The launch list may hide uncertainty about what the owner wants to put into the world, but it may also contain legitimate preparation. James asks whether the work itself remains wanted and whether showing a bounded version would offer useful lived evidence. He does not claim delay reveals fear.

**Strongest objection answered.** Seth may say recipient feedback is enough for the next step. James agrees when the owner finds the direction clear; the reflective lens should then stay quiet.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Review what making and delivering the offer felt like after novelty passed, alongside buyer outcomes and costs. Ask whether the owner wants another round for reasons they can name.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If launch enthusiasm fades because delivery is poorly designed, do not call that a loss of meaning before fixing the workflow. If the direction remains unwanted after fair experience, allow a responsible change.

### Situation 5 — A commitment already made is consuming the margin

Use [case 5](../casebook.md#case-5-a-commitment-already-made-is-consuming-the-margin) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**James's causal reading.** This is the clearest direction question, but the eight remaining workshops are a present responsibility. James separates the owner no longer choosing future editions from what current participants are owed. He asks whether the wish for another project reflects a recognized meaningful pull, ordinary fatigue or something still unclear.

**Strongest objection answered.** Exploration can become a way to avoid finishing. James concedes and makes the current obligation visible in the experiment's capacity budget; a new direction receives no automatic priority.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Ask what the owner learned from completing or renegotiating responsibly and from any actual new experience. Preserve uncertainty if neither path was sufficiently tried.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the owner abandons reliance without remedy, the advice fails even if they feel relieved. If the new project is never attempted, its imagined meaning remains untested rather than validated by dissatisfaction with the old one.

## Cases in which the seat’s default should lose

### Reversal 1 — A purely practical delay

**Situation:** An invoice cannot be sent because the owner lacks the customer's billing address.

**Reasoned correction:** Obtain the authorized missing information through the proper route. Do not ask what invoicing symbolizes.

**Check:** The absence of a meaning question is a valid reason for this seat to add little.

### Reversal 2 — The owner rejects the interpretation

**Situation:** The owner says the proposed role conflict does not describe their experience.

**Reasoned correction:** Accept the correction, retain their words and return to practical options. Do not intensify the interpretation.

**Check:** A lens that cannot accept rejection is not appropriate here.

### Reversal 3 — The exciting new path fails practically

**Situation:** A wanted experiment attracts no viable demand and exceeds the available budget.

**Reasoned correction:** Keep its reported significance distinct from commercial feasibility. Redesign, keep it private or stop the business version responsibly.

**Check:** Meaning cannot establish demand or cancel a financial floor.

### Reversal 4 — Ordinary work is enough

**Situation:** The owner values a stable service because it supports relationships and time outside work.

**Reasoned correction:** Accept that choice without searching for a hidden larger vocation. Help preserve the arrangement and obligations.

**Check:** A quiet chosen purpose needs no dramatic narrative.

## A direction question needs experience of the direction

An owner reports that a technically successful service feels less chosen and asks to explore a different role. A new role can be attractive because it is meaningful, because it is unfamiliar, or because its practical burdens are not yet visible. James cannot determine which account is true from the attraction itself. Specify an experience that includes an ordinary part of the role: preparation, responsibility, waiting, correction or collaboration. A ceremonial visit that shows only recognition would give weak evidence about living that work.

The recommendation is a feasible encounter with the role, accompanied by the owner’s own account afterward. It may be a supported guest task or observation where participation is actually permitted. No irreversible role change is required merely to learn. Keep its cost inside available resources and existing duties. An experiment that transfers uncertainty to an unsupported employee has failed the responsibility condition even if the owner reports renewed energy.

Ask what the owner noticed during the unglamorous parts, what they would willingly repeat, and which costs were surprising. Do not compress the answers into a numerical meaning score. They may value an experience while deciding against making it a business. They may also choose ordinary paid work because it supports a meaningful life elsewhere. The operational implication follows their stated choice and practical evidence, not an assumption that personally significant work must become the main source of income.

## Interpretations that must be able to disappear

Suppose an owner repeatedly takes delegated decisions back. One account is that their old role feels familiar; another is that the employee lacks necessary authority or training. First inspect what the employee was allowed to decide, the support offered and the actual errors. If corrected handovers resolve the pattern, the speculative role interpretation should disappear. It has not become a deeper truth merely because it sounded plausible.

If the practical arrangement works and the owner still reports an unresolved pull to remain central, ask whether examining that experience is useful to them. Their rejection is a stopping condition, not evidence of denial. Accepting a question once does not require them to continue an unwanted inquiry indefinitely. The aim is a clearer chosen action, not an increasingly elaborate account of personality.

The strongest objection to James’s approach is that reflection can delay an obvious decision. Take it seriously. If all available readings support the same sensible price change, make that operational recommendation without waiting for a final account of what charging means. Reflection may accompany a real quoting experience if invited. It cannot be allowed to hide a missing cost calculation, a clear delivery failure or a duty requiring prompt action.

At day 60 retain exact reported experience alongside what was actually attempted. Initial excitement followed by fatigue can mean ordinary effort, a bad workflow or an unwanted direction; it does not settle among them. Ask about concrete episodes and exceptions. If the owner would choose the direction again with fuller knowledge and responsibilities remained supported, that is useful early evidence. It does not certify a lasting purpose. If the alternative was never experienced, its imagined appeal remains unresolved rather than proved by dissatisfaction with the current work.
