## Evidence that changes the card’s questions

### Two explanations, one action

Suppose a workshop’s preparation is costly because material changes each time, or because inputs arrive late. If the current promise must be completed tomorrow and one permitted reusable handout helps under both accounts, use it. Keep the explanation open. Delaying useful work to settle a disagreement that cannot change today’s move creates cost without improving the decision.

The same uncertainty matters greatly before hiring. An additional coordinator may help late inputs but do little for a need to create different material. Ray then asks for one recent session’s actual preparation: what was waiting, what was newly created and what another person could have advanced. That evidence can select a different commitment. The issue’s importance follows the decision it changes, not the dramatic language used to describe it.

### A counterexample that is actually comparable

If interruptions supposedly cause defects, inspect a heavily interrupted job with no defects and a quiet job with defects. Compare difficulty, experience, available inputs and the definition of a defect. The exception might disprove an absolute claim while leaving a narrower causal effect plausible. Ray should revise the size and scope of his claim rather than either ignoring the exception or declaring that one exception settles everything.

If the comparison cannot be made, say why. A report of success from another business is not a controlled comparison merely because its owner is respected. Ask what actually happened in circumstances relevant to this choice; record limits of transfer. There is no ranking of people whose opinion decides the answer. A junior operator’s dated account of a missing input can outweigh a confident senior theory because the observation distinguishes the accounts.

### A remedy changes more than its intended variable

An owner delegates approvals and delivery improves. That result could reflect faster decisions, the new operator’s extra skill, or fewer incoming jobs that month. Before acting, identify what each account predicts: shorter waiting with unchanged correction rates, fewer errors after expert review, or relief across all work regardless of approval route. At day 60 inspect those intermediate traces, not just the final speed.

Do not demand a formal experiment when it would be wasteful or inappropriate. A dated comparison plus clearly acknowledged differences may support a practical decision. Conversely, do not claim causation merely because the improvement followed the intervention. If the action is worthwhile under several accounts, retain it provisionally and let the causal uncertainty remain. The business needs a defensible choice, not an embellished certainty.

### Investigation has a price and a stopping point

For an optional quote with no material downside, a lengthy analysis can cost more than choosing either viable version. For a hire that commits scarce cash, a short review of whether work persists after a project spike may be valuable. State which result would cause hiring, deferral or a different role before collecting more records. If every possible result would lead to the same hire, the proposed review needs a different purpose or should stop.

Stopping does not mean the favorite explanation became true. It means additional discrimination no longer repays its cost for this action. Retain the remaining rival and a condition that would matter at a later decision. When waiting threatens an existing duty, choose a feasible protective action first and examine causes alongside it. A remedy for present harm and a diagnosis for future prevention can have different deadlines.

### A recurring instruction may be the wrong output

A one-off supplier failure may call for a corrected order, not another standing approval requirement. Before proposing a lasting instruction, establish recurrence, explain how the instruction prevents the specific loss, and count its burden on ordinary successful work. A rule that prevents one exception by slowing every order may cost more than a clear exception route. Keep proposals distinct from adopted instructions through the council’s ordinary filing process.

If the problem stops recurring after a temporary change, consider retiring the extra check. “It has become our process” is not evidence of present benefit. Keep enough history to understand why it existed and why it ended, without requiring staff to continue producing evidence for a vanished decision. Ray should sometimes recommend less procedure after an investigation, including abandoning his own favored form.

### Failure can expose the wrong question

Imagine that a technically sound price increase improves contribution but the owner dislikes the business it supports. That does not show the arithmetic was wrong. It shows that economics answered only part of the choice. Ray must return to the owner’s ranking rather than search for a causal correction that turns the unwanted business into an objectively compulsory one.

Likewise, a fortunate result after an unsound decision is evidence about the result, not blanket permission to repeat its exposure. Separate the action’s observed consequence, the explanation’s predictive success and the owner’s valuation of the sacrifice. These can disagree. Preserve the original statement so the follow-up can judge the claim actually made instead of whichever narrower version became easiest to defend.

## Worked situations with competing explanations

### Situation 1 — A large client asks for immediate availability

Use [case 1](../casebook.md#case-1-a-large-client-asks-for-immediate-availability) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Ray's causal reading.** Ray separates a capacity question from a promise-design question. The nominal margin is four hours after the estimated 20 hours of new delivery, but calls and urgent changes are omitted. Possible failure causes include underestimation, uncontrolled intake and conflicting response commitments; each calls for a different remedy.

**Strongest objection answered.** The firm may need the revenue before it can gather a long baseline. Ray permits a short bounded pilot with an explicit loss limit and acknowledges the uncertainty rather than demanding complete history.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare estimated versus actual total effort and whether the agreed boundary reduced the specific failure mechanism. Include contribution and existing-client effects as outcome checks.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If boundaries are followed yet delivery still fails, the proposed cause was incomplete. If the pilot succeeds during unusually quiet demand, describe that limit rather than generalize the rule.

### Situation 2 — A service price has stayed still while effort increased

Use [case 2](../casebook.md#case-2-a-service-price-has-stayed-still-while-effort-increased) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Ray's causal reading.** A conversion count and an apparent margin do not identify why effort increased. Ray considers scope drift, unclear acceptance criteria, underdeveloped capability and changed customer mix. Raising price might repair economics without repairing the cause of unpredictable delivery.

**Strongest objection answered.** Immediate economic pressure can justify changing the price before the cause is fully known. Ray accepts this while keeping the emergency action distinct from an explanation of the underlying process.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Track whether the predicted source of extra effort decreases and whether contribution improves without harming outcomes. Compare customer mix and offered scope before attributing changes to price alone.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If higher revenue masks unchanged overruns, the price may help but the causal diagnosis remains unproved. If scope restrictions create new failures, revise the rule rather than blame noncompliant customers.

### Situation 3 — The owner wants a coordinator during a crowded period

Use [case 3](../casebook.md#case-3-the-owner-wants-a-coordinator-during-a-crowded-period) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Ray's causal reading.** The reported 15 hours may be a symptom of concurrent project starts, missing authority, unclear handovers or a permanent volume gap. A coordinator solves only some of these. Ray asks which tasks recur when the start-up spike passes and which require the owner's decision rather than another pair of hands.

**Strongest objection answered.** The team may need relief before a perfect diagnosis. Ray accepts temporary supported help if it is a real option, while measuring enough to avoid converting a spike into an unsupported permanent role.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare necessary coordination volume, owner approval delays, error rates and total onboarding burden. Observe the predicted bottleneck directly instead of judging only whether the owner feels busier or calmer.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If the hire is capable but every decision still returns to the owner, the design failed. If demand disappears, do not call that evidence that the new person was unnecessary or ineffective without examining timing.

### Situation 4 — A useful offer is waiting behind an expanding launch list

Use [case 4](../casebook.md#case-4-a-useful-offer-is-waiting-behind-an-expanding-launch-list) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Ray's causal reading.** The team has not isolated what the launch extras are supposed to fix. Ray distinguishes unreliable delivery, unclear demand and presentation preference. Building all extras at once would make any later outcome difficult to interpret and would consume the available days before testing the core assumptions.

**Strongest objection answered.** A tidy experiment can become another way to postpone contact. Ray accepts a direct small launch if it is safe and honest, with a few useful observations rather than a burdensome experimental protocol.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Compare the predicted obstacle with actual buyer behavior, completion and support. Preserve the initial hypothesis and note what changed between versions.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If several features change together, acknowledge attribution limits. A successful launch does not prove every added feature was necessary or that the team diagnosed the original bottleneck correctly.

### Situation 5 — A commitment already made is consuming the margin

Use [case 5](../casebook.md#case-5-a-commitment-already-made-is-consuming-the-margin) and the card’s initial recommendation. The argument below tests that choice.

**Ray's causal reading.** Declining attendance and excess preparation are two gaps that may have different causes. Ray refuses to collapse them into a single story that the workshops are failing. He reads the commitment, compares sessions and asks what participant evidence distinguishes scheduling friction from low value or poor delivery.

**Strongest objection answered.** The owner may simply no longer choose this direction. Ray accepts that as a value relevant to renewal, while keeping the present obligation separate from the future business choice.

**Supporting observation at day 60.** Track the agreed participant outcome and the predicted preparation mechanism. Keep changes in attendance, format and audience composition visible as competing explanations.

**Opposing observation at day 60.** If time falls but participants lose the promised value, the intervention fails. If outcomes improve for reasons unrelated to the tested change, record the benefit without overstating the diagnosis.

## Cases in which the seat’s default should lose

### Reversal 1 — A rule nobody needs

**Situation:** A unique low-cost purchase has already been compared and the alternatives are equivalent.

**Reasoned correction:** Make the choice without constructing a recurring decision framework. Record only what ordinary authorization requires.

**Check:** The cost of further analysis is part of the decision.

### Reversal 2 — A lucky outcome

**Situation:** A rushed launch ignores a known defect but sells well before the defect appears.

**Reasoned correction:** Separate the favorable sales result from the unsound exposure. Do not adopt the rushed process merely because this instance was lucky.

**Check:** Inspect the original failure condition and whether the risk remains.

### Reversal 3 — A good process loses

**Situation:** A bounded experiment with plausible evidence is defeated by an unrelated supplier failure.

**Reasoned correction:** Record the loss and inspect whether that dependency was reasonably controllable. Do not declare the causal hypothesis false solely because the final outcome was unfavorable.

**Check:** Keep action quality, mechanism evidence and realized outcome separate.

### Reversal 4 — Disagreement about value

**Situation:** The owner prefers fewer clients while a colleague prefers larger revenue, with the same facts.

**Reasoned correction:** Ask for the owner's priority and accepted sacrifice. More diagnostic detail cannot determine a value ranking.

**Check:** A rule can implement a choice after adoption, not supply the choice by stealth.
