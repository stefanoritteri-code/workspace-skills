# Argument, evidence, and originality

> Reference for writing-pack-substack-newsletter. Read when outlining claims, checking evidence, or adapting a reference.

A strong newsletter combines an owned point of view with evidence appropriate to the claim. Research should increase the article's truth, specificity, and usefulness. It should not bury the owner's perspective or make the prose sound like a report assembled from search results.

## Four claim classes

Label important claims during outlining:

- **Owned experience:** what the owner observed, did, felt, learned, or can demonstrate from their own work.
- **External fact:** a verifiable statement about the world, research, history, a creator, a market, or performance.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from evidence that remains open to alternatives.
- **Opinion or principle:** a judgment, preference, value, or recommendation the writer is willing to defend.

Do not present an inference as a fact or invent owned experience to make a draft feel personal. Verify consequential facts. If evidence is unavailable, narrow the statement, qualify it, or mark the gap for them.

## Build the argument spine

An article should have one main thesis and a small number of supporting claims. For each section, work through:

1. the claim;
2. the reason the reader should care;
3. the proof or example;
4. the strongest counterpoint;
5. the transition created for the next section.

This spine is a thinking tool applied internally, not a set of output fields: the outline shown to the owner carries one line per section, the claim and its proof.

Place evidence near the claim it supports. Explain what a statistic, study, example, or source changes. A pile of links is not reasoning. The article can remain exploratory, but its uncertainty should be legible.

## Three evidence lanes

Keep source roles distinct:

- **Their lane:** the owner's notes, stories, examples, results, prior writing, audience context, and voice.
- **Market lane:** current Substack posts, performance as read, recurring reader problems, title families, and saturation.
- **Structural lane:** reference articles used to study editorial mechanisms.

A structural reference is not evidence for a factual claim unless its underlying source supports that claim. A high-performing post shows that an execution resonated for one publication; it does not prove universal demand or causation.

## Original adaptation

Before using a reference, name the mechanism being borrowed in abstract terms: coined-concept diagnosis, personal-to-framework progression, identity-led contrarian argument, or science-to-application explainer. Then rebuild the article from the owner's thesis, evidence, examples, audience, and language.

Do not copy distinctive phrases, metaphors, scene order, anecdotes, claims, worldview, or cadence. Do not impersonate a creator or imply endorsement. If the draft feels derivative, change the underlying argument or proof rather than merely swapping words.

Headline and subject-line syntax is the exception. A proven title frame is shared craft: reuse the shape and fill it with their own claim, numbers, and story. What must not travel is the source's substance — its figures, names, result, and distinctive phrasing.

## Research receipts

Show only sources that were actually read. State what each source contributed: factual support, counterargument, market signal, structural mechanism, or context about them. Keep receipts and proof gaps outside the publishable body unless citations belong in the article itself. If the owner rejects a source, stop relying on it and rerun the affected reasoning.

The final standard is not novelty at any cost. It is an article whose central claim, evidence, examples, and expression belong together and could not have been produced by copying the surface of a successful reference.
