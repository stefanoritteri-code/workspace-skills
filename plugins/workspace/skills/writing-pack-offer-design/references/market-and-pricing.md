# Market selection and pricing posture

> Reference for writing-pack-offer-design. Read when judging the market or setting the price.

The offer can only be as good as the market it addresses. Before constructing components, judge the market on four traits, and let the judgment shape the blueprint's positioning section.

## The four market traits

1. **Pain** — the market must feel the problem acutely enough to pay to end it. A vitamin market (nice to have) supports weaker offers than a painkiller market (must solve). If the audience is problem-unaware, the offer's framing must first make the pain concrete — which is harder and costlier than selling into felt pain.
2. **Purchasing power** — the audience must be able to pay what the offer needs to charge. A perfect offer to an audience that cannot afford it is a hobby. Flag this early: if the interview reveals a broke audience, the honest recommendations are a cheaper delivery mechanism, a different segment of the same market, or a different market.
3. **Ease of targeting** — the audience must be findable: they gather somewhere, search for something, follow someone. An offer for a diffuse audience spends its margin on acquisition.
4. **Growth** — a growing market forgives imperfect offers; a shrinking one punishes good ones. Note direction, not just size.

Niching multiplies price. The same mechanism sold to "everyone" commands commodity pricing; narrowed to a specific person with a specific problem in a specific situation, it commands a premium — because perceived likelihood ("this was built for people exactly like me") jumps. When an offer feels weak, narrowing the WHO is usually cheaper than strengthening the WHAT.

## Pricing posture

- Price communicates. A premium price raises perceived value and attracts customers who act; a low price attracts customers who don't, and starves the delivery quality that makes the outcome real. Default to pricing on value delivered (a fraction of the quantified outcome), never on cost-plus or competitor-matching.
- The gap between price and perceived value is the offer. The blueprint's job is to widen that gap by raising perceived value — never by racing the price down.
- If the owner resists premium pricing, do not argue ideology. Show the arithmetic: the quantified outcome, what capturing a fraction of it is worth, and what the current price says about the promise.

## Rules

- Market judgments made from the interview are inferences — mark them as suggestions and say what evidence would confirm them.
- Do not recommend switching markets casually; it resets everything. Recommend it only when pain or purchasing power is genuinely absent.
- This is offer design, not financial advice: pricing recommendations are positioning judgments for the owner to weigh.
