# Assumption and contradiction mapping

> Reference for writing-pack-idea-development. Read when pressure-testing a belief or a decision.

Pressure-testing should improve a belief, not reward reflexive disagreement. Surface only the assumptions that materially affect the conclusion, and test them with the owner's evidence, a realistic counterexample, or an alternative frame.

## Four useful assumption types

### Structural assumptions

These describe how a system, market, relationship, or domain is organized. Examples include who has power, what the bottleneck is, which constraints are fixed, and which actors can respond. Test a structural assumption by changing the map: who else matters, which constraint is actually movable, and what mechanism connects the parts?

### Causal assumptions

These claim that one thing produces another. Separate correlation, plausible mechanism, necessary condition, and sufficient cause. Ask what else could explain the outcome, whether the cause precedes the effect, and what observation would make them revise the claim.

### Value assumptions

These determine which outcomes count as good, important, fair, or worth the cost. Value disagreement often hides beneath factual debate. Name the tradeoff without pretending values can be settled by evidence alone. Ask what the owner is optimizing and what the owner is willing to sacrifice.

### Boundary assumptions

These determine what is inside or outside the frame: time horizon, audience, scale, context, and exceptions. Many apparent contradictions disappear when the boundary becomes explicit. Ask where the claim applies, where it stops, and whether the exception is noise or evidence that the claim needs refinement.

## Contradictions are diagnostic

When two statements conflict, consider four possibilities:

1. One claim is wrong.
2. Each claim applies in a different context.
3. The owner values two outcomes that genuinely trade off.
4. A missing distinction or higher-order principle can hold both.

Name the contradiction neutrally. Quote or cite the actual claims when they come from their notes. Ask which of the four explanations seems most plausible, then test it. Do not diagnose hypocrisy or hidden motives.

## Steelman before rebuttal

Construct the strongest reasonable opposing view, not an easy caricature. State what evidence supports it, which value it protects, and where it is most likely to be correct. Then compare it with their position. The purpose is to reveal the true disagreement and improve the final claim.

## Evidence that changes the answer

For important conclusions, ask what would change their mind. Useful revision criteria are observable and proportionate: a counterexample, a failed prediction, a different audience response, a cost threshold, or a newly discovered constraint. Avoid demanding impossible certainty.

## Pressure-test output

A compact pressure test should leave them with:

- the current claim;
- the assumption carrying the most weight;
- the strongest opposing view;
- the evidence supporting each side;
- the boundary or distinction that improves the claim;
- what would change the conclusion;
- the practical implication now.

If the owner is deciding rather than theorizing, translate the map into criteria and tradeoffs. If the owner is developing an idea, translate it into a sharper thesis with honest limits.
