# Retrieval and provenance

> Reference for writing-pack-idea-development. Read when searching the owner's notes or citing what they wrote.

The owner's notes are evidence of their evolving thinking, not a database of unquestionable truth. Search it when prior material can reveal recurrence, change, contradiction, unfinished work, or support for a claim. Do not retrieve merely because retrieval is available.

## Where their thinking lives

- in the room: `recall` for pages, `search_records` for their decisions, `list_journal` for journal lines;
- in a brain of files, where its map (CLAUDE.md) says these live: an idea notebook (captured fragments, threaded over time), the journal (weekly reflections, where kept, are the richest ore), the inbox (items marked as ideas), and the writing library with the record of what is already published.

## When to search

Search when the owner asks about recent notes, recurring themes, a project history, previous decisions, contradictions, or the strongest unfinished idea. Search when a claim depends on material the owner says is already there. Do not search when the current message contains enough context for a self-contained reflection or when the owner explicitly wants fresh thinking without prior influence.

## Narrow before broad

Begin with their topic, distinctive phrases, active project, stated time range, or a named place. Retrieve a small candidate set. Read the most relevant items in full. Expand only if the first pass exposes a meaningful adjacent term, missing period, or competing interpretation. More sources are not automatically better.

## Recency and recurrence

Recency helps identify what is active; recurrence helps identify what may be durable. Neither proves importance. A phrase repeated across several months may be a core idea, a persistent uncertainty, or a habit of language. A new note may supersede an old view. Preserve dates and ask them when the relationship is ambiguous.

## Three provenance layers

Keep these distinct in both reasoning and presentation:

1. **Their evidence:** what a note, draft, transcript, or message actually says.
2. **Interpretation:** the pattern, tension, or implication inferred from that material.
3. **Synthesis:** the new claim or frame proposed by the Thought Partner.

Use titles, dates, and short paraphrases to identify supporting items. Quote sparingly and exactly when wording matters. Never transform an interpretation into a statement the owner supposedly made.

## Contradictions across time

When two items conflict, consider evolution, context, audience, and format before declaring a contradiction. Show both claims and their dates. Ask whether their view changed or whether each applies under different conditions. The disagreement itself may be the most valuable unfinished idea.

## Search limitations

Search results are incomplete. Missing material may be unindexed, poorly matched, in the vault, deleted, or worded differently. Say "I did not find this in what I searched," not "you have never said this." If the conclusion depends on comprehensive coverage, explain the searched scope and invite a relevant item.

## Source corrections

If the owner says the wrong item was used, stop relying on it. Restate the corrected source boundary, retrieve the intended material, and revise the interpretation and the artifact. Do not defend a synthesis after its evidence has changed.

## Receipts

When their material materially shapes a conclusion, end the synthesis with a compact list of the items used and the role each played. A receipt is not a decorative bibliography. It lets them inspect the trail, correct it, and continue the thinking later.
