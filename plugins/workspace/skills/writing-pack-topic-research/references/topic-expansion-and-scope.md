# Topic expansion and scope

> Reference for writing-pack-topic-research. Read when turning a broad subject into lanes or setting the boundary.

Topic research begins with a decision, not a giant keyword dump. The purpose of expansion is to reveal the distinct conversations hiding inside a broad subject, then choose a scope that can support a useful content decision.

## Start with the decision

Write down what the research must help decide. Common decisions include choosing a topic worth owning, pressure-testing an angle, finding a differentiated promise, selecting a platform-specific format, or refreshing an existing content plan. Also identify the intended audience and the time horizon. If the owner has not specified a platform, keep the first pass cross-platform. If recency is not the point, use an evergreen window with a recent-signal check rather than treating the last seven days as the whole market.

## Expand into research lanes

Turn the subject into four to eight lanes. Each lane should represent a genuinely different audience conversation, not a synonym. Useful expansion lenses are:

- **Audience problem:** the frustration, risk, or constraint people already feel.
- **Desired transformation:** the outcome or identity they want.
- **Contested belief:** the assumption creators disagree about.
- **Practical method:** the system, framework, or behavior people can apply.
- **Failure mode:** the mistake, myth, or common approach that disappoints.
- **Adjacent category:** a neighboring conversation that competes for attention.
- **Current trigger:** a technology, event, or market shift changing the stakes.
- **Format opportunity:** a conversation that behaves differently in video, long form, or short form.

For example, "self-education" can become personal curriculum design, learning versus collecting, alternatives to college, AI-assisted learning, proof of skill, and career outcomes. These are related but not interchangeable. Each can contain a different promise, audience tension, and level of saturation.

## Choose a defensible boundary

Do not research every lane equally. Run a light discovery pass, then keep the lanes that show either repeated audience interest or a plausible opening for the owner. Record excluded lanes and why they were excluded. Narrow by audience, platform, language, geography, or timeframe only when the decision requires it.

The final scope should state: the topic, the audience, the research lanes, the platforms or formats included, the time window, and the decision the evidence will inform. Treat inferred defaults as defaults, not hidden facts. If the owner changes the scope, rerun the affected comparison rather than quietly mixing incompatible evidence.

## Scope quality check

A good scope is broad enough to expose competing conversations and narrow enough to compare like with like. It does not assume that a popular keyword is a coherent market. It gives the later corpus a clear inclusion rule, permits counter-evidence, and ends with a decision the owner can act on.
