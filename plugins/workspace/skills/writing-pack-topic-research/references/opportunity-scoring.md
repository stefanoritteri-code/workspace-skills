# Content opportunity scoring

> Reference for writing-pack-topic-research. Read when ranking opportunities or pressure-testing an angle.

An opportunity is not simply a popular topic or an empty keyword. It is the intersection of demonstrated audience interest, a meaningful opening, credible fit with the owner, and a format the owner can execute.

## Scoring dimensions

Evaluate each candidate qualitatively across these dimensions:

- **Proven interest:** repeated evidence that the underlying tension or desired outcome earns attention.
- **Saturation:** how often creators repeat the same promise, structure, proof, and conclusion.
- **Evidence strength:** breadth, recency, source quality, creator coverage, and counter-signals.
- **Fit:** alignment with their audience, positioning, active projects, and platforms.
- **Owned proof:** experiences, results, examples, data, or material on their record the owner can credibly use.
- **Distinctiveness:** whether the proposed contribution changes the question, audience, evidence, consequence, or method.
- **Production feasibility:** whether the owner can make a strong version with available time (the owner's main work comes first), access, and format skills.

Do not collapse these into a fabricated decimal score. Use high, medium, low, or unclear with a short reason, then make the tradeoff visible. A high-demand opportunity with weak proof may be worse than a moderately popular opening the owner can uniquely own.

## Opportunity card

For each of three to five ranked opportunities, provide:

1. Opportunity name and one-sentence thesis.
2. Audience tension and desired payoff.
3. Market evidence and representative sources.
4. Saturation assessment and what is already overused.
5. The unclaimed or weakly served opening.
6. Why the owner can credibly own it, citing context from their record when retrieved.
7. Best initial format or platform.
8. Main risk or counter-signal.
9. The smallest useful test: a post, title package, outline, short video, or conversation prompt.

## Recommendation rule

Recommend one direction, but preserve the alternatives. Explain why the winner beats the runner-up and what new evidence would change the decision. If all opportunities are weak, say so and propose a narrower scope or adjacent topic rather than forcing a confident recommendation.

## Pressure-testing an existing angle

When the owner arrives with an angle, treat it as a candidate, not a command. Identify the underlying tension, find comparable market clusters, test whether its conclusion is saturated, look for counter-evidence, and assess their proof. Improve the angle only after diagnosing what is generic, unsupported, or mismatched.

The final recommendation should help them decide what to create next without pretending research can guarantee performance.
