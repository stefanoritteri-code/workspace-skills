# Write the argument that earns the purchase

> Reference for writing-pack-product-design. Read before the sales page, headlines, what's included, and the welcome page.

## Before drafting

Read the Product Brief, the current page, one real lesson, and the product AI's instructions and outputs. Know the buyer's awareness, strongest desire, main objection, the method, the best proof, and the next action. Study the creator's own strong copy for conviction, specificity, cadence, and their words for the problem — for the owner, read their voice rules first. When adapting an existing page, keep what works. Never swap in generic software language.

(Grounding: Ogilvy on finding the specific product fact that becomes the benefit; Claude Hopkins on specific, useful, testable claims.)

## Page progression

An opening the buyer recognizes as their situation. The problem and its cost, without invented fear. Why the usual approach leaves it unsolved. The creator's actual method and the result it produces. The AI doing the buyer's real task. What's included, as benefits. The real objections, answered. The next step. Adapt the order to awareness and proof. If the storefront already renders modules, price, or standard sections, the body complements them; never repeat the curriculum as padding.

## Headlines

- **Eyebrow above a heading:** a short all-caps belief with tension. WORK IS CHANGING. CREATE OR BE CREATED. THE GREATEST SKILL. Never a label like Why now, The system, Proof, Access.
- **Heading:** title case, a plain-spoken claim, sometimes with a parenthetical aside, sometimes cocky or self-deprecating. Nobody Wants To Be A Commodity. All Other Skills Are Useless Without This. I Might Disappoint You. The Course Teaches You. The AI Writes With You.
- **Line under the heading:** one short sentence, often a question or aside. Are you irreplaceable? It's not hard to stand out and grow.
- Read only the headings in order. A scanning buyer must be able to follow the argument. Each heading earns the paragraph under it with a benefit, a supported specific claim, or a question the section answers. Specifics come from the product, never invented numbers.

(These example headings are a style register to calibrate against, not lines to reuse; the owner's voice rules win where they differ.)

## Body voice

Confident, contrarian, conversational, short punchy lines, a little funny, lots of you and your, concrete numbers from the creator's real story. A pain list is framed "If any of these sound familiar, keep reading:" followed by second-person bullets that describe the buyer's actual day. An honest non-promise builds trust ("I'm not going to promise you 100k followers").

Banned, because they read as machine-written: balanced antithesis ("Built to run, not to sit on a shelf", "X, not Y"), "It's not X, it's Y" reframes, adjective stacks, em dashes, generic label eyebrows, "unlock", "elevate", "journey", "in today's world".

Copy is not the place to hedge. Facts that aren't proven stay in the brief; what makes it to the page is stated flat.

## What's included

One line each. What it is plus why it matters. No descriptions, no process, no framework names.

- Good: Know what to write about for the next 12 weeks after one 30-minute interview with the AI.
- Good: Bonus: Content Calendar and Daily Priorities tracker to make accountability seamless.
- Bad: A comprehensive ideation framework that helps you generate a rated backlog of on-brand ideas through a structured capture process.

Same rule for the tagline (200 characters max), the AI benefit lines, and the FAQ answers. Translate mode names into what the buyer gets. Show the AI helping with the buyer's actual task, not a list of chat abilities.

On a standalone course, one line per module: what the buyer walks away with; never mention an AI or a chat. On a digital download, one line per file: what it is and what it gets them. Good: Hook Swipe File: 50 openers that stopped the scroll, sorted by the feeling they trigger. Never mention an AI, a chat, or credits; the page sells the result of using the files.

## Welcome page

The buyer just paid. Give them: a warm opening in the creator's voice, what they'll have after each module, how to go through it (do the lesson's action and its AI ask before the next lesson), what the AI does and does not do, the resources and where they live, and one line telling them exactly where to start. Under 500 words. One block per line, no blank spacer lines.

On a standalone course the welcome page says what they will have after each module, how to go through it, and where to start, and never promises an AI. On a digital download the welcome page is the whole onboarding: what each file is, what to do first, what they will have after, and where the files live. Under 300 words. A free download's welcome page ends by pointing at the paid product.

## Proof and congruence

Testimonials stay verbatim with attribution and formatting. Put proof beside the claim it supports. An endorsement of the creator is not proof a buyer got a result; never turn a demo into a customer story. Check every promise against the product: drafting policy, supported jobs, prerequisites, integrations, materials, costs, access, support, refunds. If the AI refuses work the page promises, fix the AI or the promise before anything goes live.
