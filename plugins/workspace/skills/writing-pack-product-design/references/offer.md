# Find the buying situation, then build the offer around it

> Reference for writing-pack-product-design. Read before research, the offer, and the price.

Start with a person in a concrete situation: the progress they want, what they have tried, and what makes them look for a solution now. Read supplied customer language, current competitor pages, and first-hand accounts. Customer statements are qualitative evidence, not a survey.

## Bounded research

Study three to five real alternatives: direct products, services, free information, and plain AI. Read their actual promises, demonstrations, price and terms, input requirements, and support. Note what buyers complain about and why they choose each one. Include one credible competing approach, not only weak ones. Capture URLs and dates.

Use social research for language and interest and ads for active commercial messaging. Public prices don't reveal sales; long-running ads suggest spend, not profit; followers are not buyers. If the corpus is thin, make one focused retry, then report the gap and work from what you have. Missing data is not proof of an empty market.

## Two questions about the buyer

What does this buyer already understand: the problem, the solutions that exist, this product, or the terms of buying? And which claims has this market heard so often it no longer believes them? Answer both from evidence, provisionally. Open with the desire the buyer already has, then build what they're missing. In a crowded market, show a different process, proof, or context rather than a bigger promise. (Grounding: Eugene Schwartz's awareness and sophistication — the same diagnosis `writing-pack-landing-page-copy` uses.)

## Three candidates, one recommendation

When discovery is open, present three different buyer/outcome/delivery combinations. For each: a one-sentence promise, the creator's fit, the alternative it replaces, the AI's job, the likely friction, one small validation step. Recommend the one with the strongest mix of real expertise, painful recurring work, useful AI help, reachable buyers, and manageable delivery. Do not invent market sizes or guarantee demand. An existing course doesn't need three new directions unless its core offer is in doubt.

## Value and obstacles

Raise the outcome and the buyer's belief they'll reach it; cut avoidable time and effort. This is a diagnostic, not a formula (it is the value equation — `writing-pack-offer-design` goes deeper). List the obstacles before starting, while doing the work, and after the first result. For each: what stops the buyer, the proposed help, the observable output, what the creator must supply, the ongoing cost. Choosing an idea may need an interview and examples; judging a draft needs a rubric and critique; consistent execution needs a reusable plan. A bonus that removes no obstacle is a candidate for removal.

Course for a skill learned in order, library for recurring reference, tool for a repeatable job. Do not force every creator into a big course or a subscription.

## The complete offer

Record buyer, outcome, starting requirements, scope, method, deliverables, AI help, support, access period, update policy, exclusions, and confirmed price. Say what happens after the first useful output. If the AI relies on connected accounts, source data, or paid usage, verify it and explain the consequence.

Recommend a price range with a reason: the value of the problem, real alternatives, delivery burden, current proof, the chosen pilot. Distinguish facts from proposed terms. Never state unlimited usage, credits, lifetime updates, or human coaching unless configured and confirmed.

Risk reversal only when the creator can fulfill it: eligibility, obligations, claim process, cost, then ask for a decision. Deadlines and caps only with a real reason. Missing terms stay out of live copy.

## Differentiation without theater

Explain why this method suits this buyer with a real example or demonstration. A name like "the Portfolio Selection System" is worth something only if the selection rules exist. No fictional exclusivity, arbitrary crossed-out values, or unearned authority. Strong copy can sell a simple product whose usefulness is visible.

## Claim ledger

In the brief, separate observed facts, creator-reported facts, market hypotheses, and your recommendations. A competitor's stated price is observed; "buyers find it expensive" is a hypothesis until feedback supports it. Marketing makes clear supported claims; unresolved assumptions stay internal.

This is product and offer design, not financial advice: prices and terms are recommendations for the creator to decide.
