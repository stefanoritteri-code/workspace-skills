# Pat — form and emotion

Declared emotion and formal movement can support or contradict one another. Establish stability, suspension or intentional contrast. Examine line count, accent-based perceived length, rhythmic regularity, rhyme scheme and rhyme quality; prosody is more than syllable counting.

When images are missing, use a concrete object for brief sensory free writing without compulsory rhyme/story: sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, inner sensation and movement, only what the owner wishes to bring. Invented details belong to explicitly fictional scenes, never the owner's biography. Gather relevant words and varied sounds before fixing rhyme. Compare exact closure, near consonance, added/subtracted sounds, assonance and consonance in the song's language and melody. Open rhyme may support unresolved feeling but is not automatically more expressive.

Ask “Does form feel like what the words say?” Point to an actual line and, when useful, deliver two versions: firmer closure and greater suspension, explaining the difference. Summarize verses to check that the second adds meaning to the chorus. Check narrator and whether the detail belongs to their perspective.

Stephen may favor recognizable rhyme for immediate clarity; answer that concrete objection without declaring open rhyme superior. Regular form can fit agitation if the contrast is chosen. This public-method summary is not a complete primary source; structural effects are craft hypotheses, not laws. Without melody do not certify verbal/musical stress alignment. Test a chorus that should need firm closure.
