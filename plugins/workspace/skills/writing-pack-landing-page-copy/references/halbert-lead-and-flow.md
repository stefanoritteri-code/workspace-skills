# Halbert's lead and reading flow

> Reference for writing-pack-landing-page-copy. Read when writing or fixing the lead, the rhythm, or the transitions.

Gary Halbert's discipline, distilled: write to one person, make the first lines impossible to stop reading, and grease every sentence so the reader slides to the next.

## Write to one reader

A landing page is a letter to a single person, not an announcement to a crowd. "You," specific and singular, in a voice like a smart friend across a kitchen table who happens to know this problem cold. The moment the copy sounds like it is addressing an audience — "many of our customers," "people often ask" — it stops converting. Read every draft asking: could this be a letter?

## The lead's only job

The first lines after the headline exist to keep the reader reading — nothing else. No throat-clearing, no company backstory, no restating the headline. Enter the conversation already happening in the reader's head: the frustration they felt this morning, the thing they tried that failed, the scene they recognize instantly. Amplify the problem honestly — sharpen what it costs them in money, time, and self-respect — before any mention of the solution. A reader who feels precisely understood grants attention; one who smells a pitch leaves.

## The greased slide

Every element earns the next line's reading:

- Vary sentence length deliberately. Short sentences punch. Then a longer sentence carries the reader forward with rhythm and detail before the next short one lands.
- Use open loops honestly: raise a question the reader wants answered and answer it a few lines later. Withholding forever is a trick; paying off quickly builds trust and momentum.
- White space is part of the writing. A wall of text reads as work; short paragraphs with air between them read as easy. One-line paragraphs are legal and powerful.
- Transitions pull: end sections on momentum ("and that is when it clicked," "here is where it gets interesting") — not on summaries that give the reader a place to stop.

## Voice and honesty

Halbert's voice earns its directness by being right, not loud. Enthusiasm is allowed only where the substance backs it; urgency is allowed only where the deadline is real. The confession and the flaw ("this is not for everyone — here is who should NOT buy") convert because they are credible, and they are only credible when true.

## Rules

- Read the draft aloud in your head; anywhere the rhythm stumbles or a sentence sounds like copy instead of a person, rewrite it.
- The lead never mentions price, features, or the product name in its first lines — the reader has not granted that attention yet.
